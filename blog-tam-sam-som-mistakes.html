TAM SAM SOM Mistakes Lenders Spot First

Published July 25, 2026

Seven market-sizing mistakes, practical fixes and a fictional example that connects the opportunity to the sales forecast.

By Atul Jagga · July 25, 2026

TAM SAM SOM is where projections lose credibility fastest: a large market figure means little if the plan cannot explain who will buy, how they will be reached and what the business can deliver. Use our TAM SAM SOM guide for the full method; this article focuses on the mistakes that make market estimates difficult to defend in a lending discussion.

1. Calling the whole national industry your TAM

What it looks like: The plan quotes national industry revenue and treats every dollar as demand for its own offer. The category may include products, buyers or transactions the business will never serve. A local specialist then appears to compete for spending that has little connection to its actual customer.

The fix: Define the product, paying customer, spending category and period before choosing a source. Explain why the category fits and what you excluded. Distinguish annual customer spending from supplier revenue, transaction value or the number of businesses. Those measures are not interchangeable.

A national figure can provide context, but it needs a transparent bridge to the relevant opportunity. Keep the original category definition beside the calculation so a reader can reproduce the narrowing process. If the evidence cannot isolate your segment, label the estimate and explain the uncertainty instead of presenting it as a measured total.

2. Relying on a top-down percentage alone

What it looks like: “We only need a small share” replaces a sales argument. In a fictional, illustrative pitch, claiming 1% of a $1 billion market produces $10 million of revenue on paper. It says nothing about the customers, sales effort or operating resources needed to earn that amount.

The fix: Add a bottom-up check using reachable prospects, supported conversion assumptions, purchase frequency and average spending. Separate enquiries from paying customers and new accounts from repeat orders. Explain how the marketing budget produces those opportunities and how long conversion takes.

Compare that calculation with the top-down estimate using the same product scope and time period. A gap is useful information: it may expose an unrealistic conversion rate, duplicated customers or different definitions of spending. Investigate the disagreement rather than adjusting a percentage until the totals match.

3. Defining SAM by geography alone

What it looks like: Everyone inside a delivery radius becomes part of the serviceable available market. The plan overlooks affordability, buying authority, eligibility, required features and the willingness to use the proposed ordering channel. Being nearby does not automatically make someone a buyer.

The fix: Apply buying and serviceability filters as well as location. For a business service, that might mean the right company size, purchasing budget, equipment compatibility and contract renewal window. For a consumer offer, it might mean the relevant need, price range and practical access to the service.

Document the evidence behind each filter and avoid multiplying overlapping percentages as though they were independent. Our guide to identifying your target market helps define the buyer before counting the opportunity. Keep future expansion areas separate from the market the current business model can actually serve.

4. Choosing SOM without channels or capacity

What it looks like: The serviceable obtainable market is a convenient share of SAM, with no route from launch to sales. The plan names social media or referrals but gives no acquisition assumptions. It also ignores available staff, appointment slots, equipment throughput and delivery time.

The fix: State the SOM period and build a channel-based customer acquisition schedule. Show the prospects each channel can reach, the assumed conversion, the sales cycle and any expected losses. Avoid counting the same prospect twice across channels. Distinguish a launch-year revenue forecast from a later annual run rate.

Then test the result against capacity. Include hiring dates, training, downtime, administration and seasonal constraints. Capacity is a ceiling, not proof of demand. Where either acquisition or delivery is uncertain, show a lower scenario and explain which spending decisions depend on achieving the base case.

5. Letting market sizing contradict the financial forecast

What it looks like: The market section describes one customer count and annual spend, while the income statement assumes another. Revenue may include customers outside SAM or a full year of purchases from accounts acquired late in the year. Prices sometimes change between sections without explanation.

The fix: Maintain one set of customer, price, frequency and timing assumptions. Reconcile forecast revenue to customers served and units sold, then check the labour, purchasing and marketing costs needed to support those sales. Explain whether spending figures include sales tax and use the same treatment throughout.

Show a short bridge from the SOM estimate to monthly revenue. Allow for cancellations, discounts, ramp-up and seasonality where relevant. If SOM describes a future steady year, say so explicitly and build the first-year forecast separately. Updating the market narrative should trigger a review of the financial model.

6. Leaving sources unnamed or using decade-old data

What it looks like: A chart says “industry research” without a publisher, publication date or definition. Another figure comes from an old report but is presented as current. Readers cannot tell whether the number describes the right location, customer group or period.

The fix: Record the publisher, report or dataset title, release date, observation period and relevant table or page. Keep a retrievable reference and explain any calculations. A publication date alone is insufficient when the underlying survey covers an earlier period.

Older evidence can still inform context, but describe its limitations and seek a current cross-check. Distinguish published observations from your own interviews, estimates and assumptions. Where evidence is thin, feasibility study services can help structure further investigation before the plan relies on an untested demand estimate.

7. Forecasting market growth while the cited data declines

What it looks like: The narrative promises an expanding opportunity while its own source shows a flat or shrinking industry. The plan may confuse rising prices with growing sales volumes, or borrow a trend from a different region. The contradiction remains unexplained.

The fix: Separate industry growth, segment performance and the business’s proposed share gains. A business can plan to win customers in a flat market, but it must explain why buyers would switch and what competitors might do in response. A growing niche needs evidence specific to that niche.

Check whether the cited measure is nominal revenue, inflation-adjusted spending, units or customer counts. Align the forecast with that definition and test a flat or declining demand scenario. Where your local research differs from broader data, state the difference and its limits rather than quietly ignoring the inconvenient source.

A fictional local example: both methods reconcile

Illustrative only: Maple Desk Care is a fictional local office-cleaning business. Every number below is invented, round and illustrative; none is a real market statistic or evidence of demand. The defined offer is one annual cleaning package priced at $10,000 per office.

Illustrative top-down: Assume a fictional regional spending pool of $10 million for that exact package: TAM. Filtering for location, office type, budget and service requirements leaves $2 million: SAM, equivalent to 200 eligible offices. A proposed SOM of 10 offices is $100,000 for the stated first full operating year.

Illustrative bottom-up: A fictional prospect list contains those 200 eligible offices, each spending $10,000 annually, independently checking the $2 million SAM. Assume 100 distinct prospects reached before launch and 10 signed annual contracts starting at launch. Ten customers × $10,000 gives $100,000, matching the proposed SOM; the assumed conversion is a hypothesis to validate.

Illustrative reconciliation: One crew has capacity for 10 packages, so the customer plan and capacity ceiling agree. The first full operating year includes all 10 contracts for the full year; later starts would reduce that year’s revenue. Real planning would require sourced spending evidence, documented outreach and contracts or other demand validation, plus costs and cash collection assumptions.

How a lender reads it

Consistency with the revenue forecast is the real test. A lender can trace the market argument through customers, orders, pricing and operating capacity, then consider what those sales imply for cash available to repay debt. A large TAM does not establish repayment capacity.

Make assumptions easy to locate and reconcile. Explain the downside if sales arrive later or customers spend less. Frequently asked questions

TAM vs SAM vs SOM: what does each mean?

TAM is the total opportunity for the defined offer. SAM is the portion the business model can serve. SOM is the portion it can defensibly win within a stated period.

Do lenders check market sizing?

They may question sources, customer assumptions and market share, especially when these drive projected sales. Be prepared to show calculations and explain how demand supports the revenue and repayment forecast; review practices vary by lender and application.

Top-down or bottom-up — which is better?

Use both where practical. Top-down provides market context; bottom-up tests customers, channels and capacity. Compare them on the same scope and period, and investigate differences rather than forcing agreement.

How big should my SOM be?

Defensible beats big. Base SOM on the customers you can plausibly acquire and serve within the stated period. There is no universal correct percentage; evidence, sales timing, competition and capacity should determine the estimate.
