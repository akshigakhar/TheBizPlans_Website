Sample Business Plans: Free Examples by Industry

Sample business plans show how a business idea becomes a document that a lender, investor or founder can examine. A template gives you headings and questions. A sample puts a business inside that structure: customers, pricing, staffing, operating constraints and a funding request that connects to financial statements. Read the examples here to understand those connections, then build a plan around your own evidence.

The most useful lesson is often the link between two sections. A restaurant’s opening hours imply staffing costs. A contractor’s payment terms affect the cash available for materials. A consulting firm’s delivery capacity limits the fees it can earn. The illustrative financials in these samples make those relationships visible; they are teaching assumptions, not verified forecasts for your location or promises about what an owner will earn.

Lenders read a plan to understand how their money will be used and repaid. They may move from the funding request to cash flow, then back to the sales assumptions, management experience and evidence supporting the budget. A polished executive summary cannot resolve an unexplained cash shortage. Look for whether the operating story supports the numbers, whether the financing covers the actual uses of funds, and what happens if launch takes longer than expected.

Use this library as a reading guide rather than a submission shortcut. Company names, scenarios and illustrative figures belong to their examples. Your application needs real ownership details, local research, supplier quotations, appropriate licences and financial assumptions you can defend. Copying a sample’s numbers can hide the very risks a lender needs you to address. Neither a sample nor a completed plan guarantees approval, profitability or a particular financing outcome.

Start with the industry closest to your business model, then compare an adjacent example if it helps explain a difficult issue such as inventory, deposits or seasonal demand. The library below links to completed industry samples. After reading, choose a blank template, create a draft with the generator, or discuss a custom plan with our team. Each route still requires your business facts and careful review before you rely on the document.

How to use these samples

  1. Pick your industry. Choose the closest operating model, not simply the most familiar business name. A mobile operator, a leased storefront and a professional practice face different capacity and cash constraints.
  2. Read the financials section first. Identify the illustrative revenue drivers, direct costs, fixed expenses and opening investment. Follow how those assumptions affect profit, cash and the funding request.
  3. Adapt assumptions to your market. Replace sample prices, wages, supplier terms and volumes with dated evidence. Explain why your location, customer mix, experience and operating model justify each material change.
  4. The generator builds your own draft. Use the business plan generator to organize your inputs, then check its narrative and financial results against your research and financing requirements.

Keep a separate assumptions file as you read. For every important input, record the source, date, calculation and person responsible for confirming it. A supplier quote can support equipment costs; it cannot prove customer demand. A competitor’s advertised price can inform pricing research; it does not establish how many customers you can acquire. Distinguishing evidence from estimates makes the first draft easier to challenge and improve.

Read the financial story behind the example

Trace sales back to something the business can deliver

Begin with the units behind revenue. A café might use transactions and average spending; a contractor might use completed jobs; a consultant might use billable days. In each case, ask whether the illustrative volume fits the staff, hours, equipment and customer-acquisition process described in the sample. Revenue growth needs an operating explanation. Adding a larger sales number without adding capacity, demand evidence or delivery resources leaves a gap in the plan.

For your business, build the calculation from the ground up and document its limits. Consider opening days, cancellations, training time, downtime and the difference between quoted work and confirmed orders. Test how a slower start changes both income and cash. A sample can show the presentation of this logic, but only your market research and operating evidence can support the inputs you ultimately submit.

Separate profit from money available to spend

An income statement records revenue and expenses under its stated assumptions. Cash flow follows when customers pay, when suppliers must be paid and when money leaves for equipment, tax or debt. Those are different questions. Inventory can absorb cash before it generates sales, and a profitable job may still require money upfront for wages and materials. Look for these timing differences rather than judging an example solely by its final profit line.

When adapting the model, connect opening cash, financing proceeds and owner contributions to the uses of funds. Loan principal payments reduce cash even though they are not an operating expense in the income statement. Equipment purchases and depreciation also affect statements differently. Reconcile the statements together and make sure the projected closing cash balance does not conceal an unfunded shortfall during an earlier month.

Use benchmarks to question assumptions

The Canadian business plan benchmarks guide can help frame questions about margins, capacity and financing assumptions. A benchmark is a comparison point that needs context: geography, business size, service mix and the period measured can all matter. It should not be pasted into a forecast as proof that your business will achieve the same result. Keep the samples’ illustrative numbers separate from independently sourced market evidence.

Write a downside case alongside the base case. Try lower sales, delayed opening, slower collections or higher input costs, choosing changes relevant to your operation. Explain which costs can adjust and which commitments remain fixed. Then identify the practical response, such as a smaller initial investment, staged hiring or additional working capital. This turns sensitivity analysis into a decision the founder and lender can discuss.

Turn an example into an evidence-based plan

Read the executive summary after you understand the operating model and financial statements. Check that it describes the same customers, investment, funding need and milestones. If you change the forecast, update the summary and any tables that refer to it. A business plan becomes difficult to assess when one section assumes a small launch while another assumes a fully staffed operation from the first month.

Build your own research around the decision the plan must support. For a leased location, confirm permitted use, occupancy conditions and fit-out responsibilities before treating the opening budget as settled. For a service company, clarify delivery scope, payment terms and who performs the work. For inventory businesses, examine ordering quantities, lead times and returns. Record unresolved questions explicitly so an attractive narrative does not imply that due diligence is complete.

Before sharing the plan, review it with the people who own the assumptions. Ask the operator to confirm capacity, the founder to confirm equity and relevant advisers to review tax, legal or licensing questions within their expertise. Keep current supporting documents available. A lender may request additional schedules or a different projection period; use its actual requirements to shape the final submission rather than assuming any example is a universal checklist.

Sample business plans: frequently asked questions

Are these sample business plans free to read?

Yes. The industry samples linked in this library are free to read online. They explain plan structure and illustrative financial assumptions. Reading a sample does not require purchasing a custom plan. Professional writing services are a separate engagement with an agreed scope.

Can I copy a sample and submit it to a bank?

No. These samples are illustrative teaching documents. A lender file needs your real ownership details, market evidence, costs, funding request and financial assumptions. Use the structure as a guide, replace the fictional content, reconcile the statements and confirm the lender’s requirements. A plan cannot guarantee financing approval.

What is the difference between a sample, a template and the generator?

A sample shows an example business inside a plan. A template provides a blank structure for your own writing. The generator uses your inputs to build a draft. All three support preparation; none replaces research, verification or your responsibility for the final document.

How are the financials in the samples built?

The samples use illustrative operating assumptions, such as prices, volumes, staffing, equipment costs and payment timing, to explain projections and funding needs. The detail varies by sample. These figures are teaching examples rather than verified forecasts or market benchmarks. For your plan, use current quotes, documented calculations and evidence appropriate to your location and operating model.

Do you write custom plans for industries not listed?

Yes. The Biz Plans offers custom business plan writing for businesses beyond the sample library. Discuss your industry, location, operating model, financing objective and available evidence with the team so the research, financial work and deliverables can be scoped for your situation.
