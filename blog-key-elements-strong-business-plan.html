Key Elements of a Business Plan Every Entrepreneur Should Know

Published October 6, 2024

What really matters in a business plan — and how to cover it clearly and credibly.

  1. Home
  2. Blog & Learning Hub
  3. Key Elements
Updated: November 12, 2026

The elements of a business plan explain what you sell, who buys it, how you operate and whether the numbers support the strategy. The nine key elements of a business plan below form a complete starting structure; adapt their order and depth to the lender, investor or internal decision you need to support.

The Cedar Route Cycle Care snippets are fictional and illustrative, not client results, market research or recommended pricing. Use them to understand the logic, then replace every assumption with evidence from your own business.

1. Executive summary

What it must contain: State the business concept, customer need, offer, relevant team experience, financial highlights and the decision requested. Write this section last so the funding amount, launch dates and forecast agree with the full plan. For many small-business plans, one to two pages is a useful starting point, subject to the reader’s requirements.

Common mistake: Leading with a slogan or a long founder biography while hiding the funding request.

Fictional snippet: Cedar Route Cycle Care proposes mobile bicycle repairs for commuters in a defined service area. Its launch plan connects bookable technician hours to sales and identifies the capital needed before trading.

Read the executive summary structure and writing guide and its linked worked example.

2. Company description

What it must contain: Explain the legal structure, ownership, location, stage of development, business purpose and revenue model. Separate current facts from plans: a proposed lease is different from a signed lease. Describe milestones and the practical problem the company exists to solve.

Common mistake: Calling an idea an established operation or leaving ownership and operating geography unclear.

Fictional snippet: Cedar Route is a fictional owner-managed startup. It plans to serve one local catchment before considering additional routes; no location or customer contract is assumed secured.

3. Market analysis

What it must contain: Define the industry, reachable geography, priority customer segments, buying triggers and alternatives. Date and cite research, explain interview methods and distinguish observed demand from estimates. Compare competitors on factors buyers actually use, then connect the reachable market to your delivery capacity.

Common mistake: Using a national market total as proof of local demand or describing everyone as a potential buyer.

Fictional snippet: The initial customer hypothesis is commuters who cannot leave a bicycle at a shop during working hours. Interviews would test preferred appointment times, travel boundaries and willingness to pay.

Use these target market examples to turn a broad audience into a specific customer profile.

4. Organisation & management

What it must contain: Set out ownership, reporting lines, decision authority, founder responsibilities and relevant experience. Identify missing skills, hiring dates, compensation and any external advisers. Connect the staffing plan to payroll and explain cover for absence or increased demand.

Common mistake: Listing impressive titles without assigning responsibility or budgeting for the people needed to deliver sales.

Fictional snippet: The owner handles scheduling and bookkeeping oversight while a technician delivers repairs. A second technician is planned only after recurring bookings justify the added payroll.

5. Products and services

What it must contain: Describe the offer, customer benefit, pricing basis, direct costs, delivery process and limitations. Explain sourcing, quality checks, intellectual property where relevant and any permissions needed before selling. Put technical specifications in the appendix if they interrupt the main argument.

Common mistake: Claiming uniqueness without comparison or selling more than suppliers, staff or equipment can deliver.

Fictional snippet: Cedar Route offers scheduled tune-ups and separately priced replacement parts. Each booking includes an inspection and written approval before extra work; travel time reduces available repair hours.

6. Marketing and sales

What it must contain: Explain positioning, channels, budget, enquiry handling, conversion assumptions and repeat-purchase activity. Identify who sells, how long a sale takes, when customers pay and how results will be measured. Translate the funnel into monthly customers and revenue rather than treating marketing as a list of platforms.

Common mistake: Forecasting sales without acquisition costs, conversion evidence or a realistic sales cycle.

Fictional snippet: The startup would test commuter-group referrals and local search with a capped budget, track qualified enquiries through paid appointments and stop campaigns whose contribution does not cover acquisition cost.

7. Financial projections

What it must contain: Include a driver-based income statement, cash-flow forecast and balance sheet, supported by sales, payroll, capital spending, working-capital and debt schedules. State the period, currency, tax treatment and assumptions. Use monthly detail to expose launch delays and seasonal cash gaps; compare a base case with a downside case.

Common mistake: Confusing profit with cash, omitting owner pay or loan principal, or assuming growth without capacity.

Fictional snippet: Illustrative only: 80 appointments at CAD 100 produce CAD 8,000 monthly sales before sales taxes. The forecast must then deduct parts, paid labour, travel and overhead and schedule when cash is collected.

See how to connect the statements in our financial projections and financial modelling guide.

8. Funding request

What it must contain: State how much external funding is needed, its intended form and timing, owner contribution and itemized uses. Reconcile sources to uses, including working capital, and show repayment assumptions or investor milestones as appropriate. Separate requested terms from terms actually offered.

Common mistake: Asking for a round number that does not tie to equipment quotes, launch costs or the cash forecast.

Fictional snippet: Illustrative only: a CAD 50,000 launch budget comprises CAD 30,000 equipment and CAD 20,000 working capital, funded by CAD 20,000 owner equity and CAD 30,000 requested debt. Interest and principal still need a repayment schedule.

9. Appendix

What it must contain: Index evidence such as founder resumes, dated supplier quotes, research notes, licences, material agreements and detailed financial schedules. Reference each item from the relevant section. Mark drafts and pending approvals accurately and remove unnecessary personal or commercially sensitive information.

Common mistake: Attaching a document dump without explaining which claim each item supports.

Fictional snippet: Appendix A holds equipment quotations; Appendix B records customer interview questions and findings; Appendix C contains the monthly forecast and debt schedule. Each is referenced where used.

Elements of a business plan FAQs

What are the nine elements of a business plan?

They are executive summary, company description, market analysis, organisation and management, products or services, marketing and sales, financial projections, funding request and appendix. Add operating and regulatory detail where it helps explain execution.

Do all business plans need a funding request?

A funding request is necessary when seeking external capital. An internal plan can instead explain the available budget, owner funding and investment decisions. Follow the recipient’s submission requirements.

Which element should I write first?

Start with the customer, offer and operating assumptions, then build the financial model. Finalize the executive summary after the other sections so the facts and figures agree.

How detailed should each business plan element be?

Use enough detail to support the reader’s decision. Explain evidence and assumptions in the main plan and place lengthy research, quotations and schedules in an indexed appendix.

Where does the operational plan belong?

It may stand alone or sit within company description and products or services. Cover delivery workflow, staffing, suppliers, capacity, premises, quality controls and applicable permissions.

How do I check that my business plan is consistent?

Reconcile customer demand to sales, sales to operating capacity, staffing to payroll, spending to cash flow and funding sources to uses. Ensure the summary repeats the same figures and dates.
