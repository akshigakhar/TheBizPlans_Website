Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP): Loan & Business Plan Guide

Eligibility, what can be financed, loan limits, and how to apply.

The Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP) helps eligible businesses seek financing through participating lenders. A Canada Small Business Financing loan is assessed by the lender, not the government or The Biz Plans. Use this Canadian small business financing program guide to prepare the project budget, eligibility questions and documents for a lender discussion.

In practice: meeting the program rules and satisfying the lender are two separate tests. A business plan and financial projections help the lender assess the business case and repayment capacity.

Updated October 2026. Product terms, eligibility and application processes can change. Verify current details with the lender or program administrator. The Biz Plans is an independent business-plan service and is not affiliated with or endorsed by the organization discussed on this page.

The Canada Small Business Financing Program can help an eligible Canadian small business obtain financing through a participating bank, credit union or caisse populaire. The federal government shares part of the lender's risk under the program, but it does not approve the entrepreneur's application.

Still deciding which borrowing route fits? Start with our business loans in Canada guide for the option map and lender document package, then use this page for program details.

The financing decision remains with the lender.

In practice, an applicant has to address two different questions. First, does the business and the proposed use of funds fit the program rules? Second, is the lender comfortable that the business can repay the requested financing?

A business plan is mainly concerned with that second question. It gives the lender a clear account of where the money will go, how the investment is expected to generate revenue, what assumptions support the forecast and whether projected cash flow can carry the new debt. A plan cannot guarantee approval, but it can make the request easier to assess.

What is the CSBFP?

CSBFP stands for Canada Small Business Financing Program. Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada administers it, while participating financial institutions provide the financing. By sharing eligible lending risk with those institutions, the program can support financing that falls within its rules.

Risk sharing is not automatic approval. An applicant still has to meet the lender's credit standards and demonstrate a credible source of repayment. See the Government of Canada program overview for current rules.

Who qualifies for a Canada Small Business Financing loan?

RequirementWhat it generally means
Business locationThe business operates in Canada.

RevenueGross annual revenue of CAD $10 million or less, according to ISED’s small-business FAQ.
IndustryFarming businesses are excluded; investigate the Canadian Agricultural Loans Act program instead.
Use of financingThe proposed costs meet the rules for the relevant financing facility.
Lender approvalA participating lender is satisfied with the borrower, business case and repayment ability.

Meeting the program criteria does not mean the lender must approve the financing. The financial institution may consider personal and business credit, management experience, existing debt, historical performance, owner investment, available security and projected cash flow. For a startup, the lender will often spend more time testing the assumptions because there is no operating history to rely on.

Source: Government of Canada / ISED, Frequently asked questions—For small businesses, checked October 26, 2026.

Eligibility can also turn on the details of the project. A business may qualify while one or more proposed expenses do not. Confirm the current borrower and cost rules with the lender before committing funds.

Startups, operating businesses and the evidence behind eligibility

The official FAQ includes corporations, sole proprietors, partnerships and cooperatives. A startup can apply before it has an established trading history; an operating business can investigate financing for a defined investment. Neither situation establishes that a particular request will be accepted. Business structure, the actual activity and the proposed use of money need to be considered together.

For a startup, prepare an opening budget that distinguishes the purchase being financed from the cash needed to operate afterward. For an established business, explain how the investment changes capacity, staffing or customer demand compared with current results. In both cases, identify the borrowing entity consistently across registration records, purchase documents and forecasts. Ask the lender to resolve any uncertainty about the entity before you commission a large application package.

Source: Government of Canada / ISED, Frequently asked questions—For small businesses, checked October 2026. The program's revenue test concerns gross revenue, not profit; a loss-making forecast does not by itself demonstrate eligibility or repayment capacity.

How Much Can You Borrow Through CSBFP?

According to Government of Canada / ISED’s Helping small businesses get loans (checked October 26, 2026), the combined maximum is CAD $1.15 million:

  • Term loans: up to CAD $1 million.
  • Within that term-loan limit, up to CAD $500,000 for leasehold improvements and equipment; within that amount, up to CAD $150,000 for intangible assets and working capital.
  • Lines of credit: up to CAD $150,000.

These are ceilings, not approved amounts. Real-property financing sits within the overall term-loan ceiling; the other categories share the nested limits above. The limits are not separate allowances to add together. Confirm the applicable categories and any existing program borrowing with the lender.

Cost-sharing, owner contribution and borrowing costs

The federal government shares eligible losses with the lender under program rules. This protects part of the lender’s risk; it does not pay the borrower’s instalments or cancel the debt. Confirm the applicable loss-sharing rules with ISED and the lender before accepting an offer.

ISED’s Helping small businesses get loans (checked October 2026) states a 2% registration fee. The term-loan fee is based on the amount loaned; the line-of-credit fee is based on the authorized amount. The borrower pays the lender, and the fee may be financed. Interest ceilings are lender prime plus 3% for floating term loans, the lender’s single-family residential mortgage rate for the loan term plus 3% for fixed term loans, and lender prime plus 5% for lines of credit. Ask for the actual rate and total costs.

Ask the lender which project costs it will finance and what owner contribution, security and guarantees it requires. Budget for costs outside the approved facility. Do not assume government risk-sharing means the entire project will be funded. Prepare the budget in CAD and identify GST/HST separately, including expected recoveries where applicable, so the lender can confirm treatment.

There is no verified universal down-payment percentage to insert into this guide. ISED's Canada Small Business Financing Program Guidelines, section 5.1 (checked October 2026), says the financing percentage is negotiated and determined by the lender. Government loss-sharing is a different calculation from the portion of a purchase the borrower funds. Ask for the required contribution in writing rather than assuming a standard split.

The same Guidelines, sections 4.1.5 and 5.4 (checked October 2026), explain that financed registration fees count within the relevant ceilings and eligible costs exclude refundable taxes. A fee cannot simply be added above a category maximum. Keep invoices, payment evidence and the financing schedule together so the lender can reconcile them.

Illustrative example: financing a defined equipment purchase

Fictional business; every purchase, financing and contribution figure below is illustrative, not an offer or a required program percentage. Imagine a small bakery replacing its oven and mixer. The assumed purchase price is CAD $100,000, excluding refundable taxes. For this example only, the lender confirms the equipment and the full purchase amount are eligible, with no adjustments, and agrees to finance 80% of that amount. The 80% is a negotiated example assumption; it is not a CSBFP rule.

  1. Illustrative eligible equipment cost: CAD $100,000.
  2. Illustrative asset financing: CAD $100,000 × 80% = CAD $80,000.
  3. Illustrative borrower contribution to the equipment: CAD $100,000 − CAD $80,000 = CAD $20,000.
  4. Applying the verified 2% registration fee to the illustrative CAD $80,000 asset advance gives CAD $1,600. Assume the borrower pays this fee separately rather than financing it.
  5. Illustrative cash contribution for the equipment and fee: CAD $20,000 + CAD $1,600 = CAD $21,600. Illustrative project uses of CAD $101,600 equal CAD $80,000 of debt plus CAD $21,600 of borrower cash.

This arithmetic separates eligible cost, the agreed loan and the owner's cash. The illustrative CAD $80,000 equipment loan is below the verified category ceiling, but being below a ceiling does not establish approval. The calculation uses the Guidelines' negotiated-financing approach and the official registration-fee rule cited above. No grant or government payment is assumed to fund the owner's contribution.

The bakery must also budget for any tax cash outlay, delivery, installation, downtime and operating reserve; none is silently included in the illustrative CAD $21,600. Those additional items need their own eligibility review and funding source. If the quote changes, update the sources-and-uses schedule and forecast together. If the lender offers a different financing percentage, recalculate the contribution rather than treating the example as a promised outcome.

What a CSBFP loan can and cannot fund

The program can finance qualifying business assets and expenses. Common examples include machinery, commercial equipment, business vehicles, computer systems, renovations to leased premises and eligible real property used by the business. Intangible assets and working capital may also be financed within the rules and sub-limits that apply to the selected facility.

The category matters. A line of credit used for day-to-day operating costs is not treated in the same way as a term loan used to buy a building or install equipment. Nor does every expense in a project budget automatically qualify. The participating lender should confirm how each material cost is categorized before the business signs a purchase contract or spends the money.

Goodwill is not automatically excluded from every asset purchase: ISED's CSBFP Guidelines, section 4.1.4 (checked October 2026), lists goodwill as an intangible asset when part of a going-concern purchase. Have the lender review the purchase allocation and applicable limits. Do not treat a business purchase price as one undifferentiated eligible amount.

Costs outside the approved facility

A CSBFP facility is not unrestricted cash for any purpose. Personal spending and costs unrelated to the eligible business project should stay outside the request. ISED’s small-business FAQ (checked October 2026) excludes share purchases and assets acquired by a holding company. Ask the lender to check any proposed refinancing against the program rules. Separate eligible asset purchases from other acquisition costs and show another funding source for costs the lender excludes.

How to apply for CSBFP through a lender

A CSBFP application normally begins with a participating financial institution, not with the federal government. The lender receives the application, asks for the supporting documents it considers necessary and decides whether it is prepared to lend.

That review may cover the owners' credit history, management experience, existing obligations, equity contribution, available security, project costs and the business's expected ability to make its payments. A startup may need to support the application with a business plan, quotations and detailed monthly cash-flow projections. An established company may also be asked for historical and interim financial statements.

The lender conducts its own due diligence and applies its normal lending standards. The government's risk-sharing arrangement does not replace that work and does not oblige the lender to approve an otherwise eligible applicant. Our guide to how banks evaluate business plans explains the commercial questions behind this review.

If the lender approves the financing and determines that the borrower, costs and facility meet the program requirements, the financing can be registered under CSBFP. The practical distinction is important: the lender makes the credit decision, while the program establishes the rules under which eligible financing can be registered. ISED also provides a current list of participating lenders.

A practical application sequence

  1. Prepare the request. Reconcile the project budget, owner funds, supporting quotes and financial projections. Record unresolved cost questions for the lender.
  2. Approach a participating lender. Present the business proposal and ask for its checklist for the proposed facility. Confirm that it handles this kind of request before submitting sensitive documents.
  3. Respond to adjudication questions. The lender decides, not the government, and not us. Eligibility is only part of the review; answer questions about repayment, credit and the project without assuming registration is approval.
  4. Review the offer and complete the lender's conditions. Understand security, guarantees, pricing and the contribution required before committing. Keep the signed offer consistent with the forecast.
  5. Disbursement and registration. ISED's Helping small businesses get loans (checked October 2026) describes the lender disbursing funds and registering the loan after deciding to finance. Ask the lender how it will handle payments, registration and fee collection in your file.

There is no promised decision or disbursement timeline here. Build a purchase schedule around the lender's actual conditions, quote expiry dates and the business's cash resources. If information changes during review, send a revised schedule with a clear explanation of what changed, rather than leaving inconsistent versions in the file.

Do You Need a Business Plan for a CSBFP Loan?

There is no useful one-size-fits-all answer. The lender decides what it needs to evaluate a particular application. A detailed plan is more likely to be requested for a startup, acquisition, major expansion, substantial financing request or business without a long financial history.

The lender is not simply looking for a description of the company. It needs to understand how much is being requested, where the money will go, why the project makes commercial sense and how the resulting business is expected to repay the debt.

A clear and internally consistent plan gives the lender better information for that assessment. Applicants seeking a broader overview can consult our Canadian bank loan business plan guide.

What Should a CSBFP Business Plan Include?

Financing Request

State the amount requested, the intended facility, the purpose of the financing and when the funds are needed. Avoid making the reviewer reconstruct the request from several schedules.

Sources and Uses of Funds

Show the full project cost and how it will be funded. The schedule should identify requested financing, owner contribution, other financing and the use of every source. Material costs should connect to quotations, contracts or estimates.

Business and Management Background

Explain the ownership structure and the experience relevant to operating the business. If the team has a material skill gap, address how it will be filled rather than presenting a generic management biography.

Market Analysis

Define the target customer, geographic market, evidence of demand and realistic competitive position. The purpose is to support the sales assumptions, not to fill the plan with broad industry statistics.

Sales and Marketing Strategy

Explain how prospects are expected to become paying customers. Pricing, channels, sales cycles, conversion assumptions and expected customer volume should lead logically to the revenue forecast.

Operations

Describe the premises, equipment, suppliers, staffing, capacity and implementation schedule. Make clear what changes when the financing is received and who is responsible for the work.

Financial Projections

Translate the operating plan into revenue, expenses, capital spending, working capital and cash flow. The assumptions should be visible enough for a lender to understand how the forecast was built.

Repayment Capacity

Include the proposed loan proceeds, interest and principal payments. Show when operating cash flow is expected to support those payments and where temporary cash shortfalls would be funded.

Risk Analysis

Discuss the material risks honestly and explain what management can do about them. A realistic downside case is often more informative than a long list of generic risks.

What Makes a CSBFP Business Plan Different From a Regular Business Plan?

A general business plan may explain the company, its customers and its growth strategy. A financing plan has another job: it must connect that strategy to a specific request for money.

A lender should be able to trace the requested amount through quotations, equipment purchases, the renovation budget, working-capital allowance, sources-and-uses schedule and projections. If those records show different totals, reconcile them before submitting the application.

Eligible and Other Project Costs

The complete project budget may include costs that are not eligible under the selected facility. The plan should still show the whole budget, identify which costs are expected to be financed through the requested loan and explain how the rest will be paid.

Sources and Uses of Funds

Every use of money should have a source. Requested financing, owner investment, other debt and available business cash should reconcile to the full project cost.

Owner Contribution

If the owner is contributing cash or assets, the amount and timing should be clear. The forecast should not quietly depend on additional owner funding that is absent from the written financing structure.

Debt Service and Repayment Capacity

The projections need to include the proposed debt, interest and principal payments. A profitable income statement is not enough if the monthly cash-flow forecast shows that the business will run short of cash before customer receipts arrive.

That reconciliation is the main difference. The narrative, project budget, supporting documents and forecast should describe the same transaction.

Financial Projections for a CSBFP Loan

A projected income statement can show a profit while the company still runs short of cash. A business may have to pay suppliers, buy inventory, meet payroll and make loan payments before its customers pay their invoices. That timing is why lenders often pay close attention to cash flow.

Useful projections commonly address sales volume and pricing, cost of goods sold, payroll, operating expenses, capital expenditures, opening working capital, cash receipts and payments, debt service and break-even. A projected balance sheet may also be appropriate, particularly when the request includes material assets and debt. The period and level of detail depend on the business and the lender.

The forecast should be consistent with the written plan:

  • When employees are hired, payroll should change in the same forecast period.
  • If equipment is purchased before opening, capital spending, cash flow and the asset balance should reflect that purchase.
  • If loan proceeds fund the purchase, the financing assumptions should show the advance, interest and repayments on the same timing basis.

Revenue deserves the same discipline. If the forecast depends on a target monthly customer volume, the market, sales and operating sections should explain how the business expects to attract and serve that volume. Documents to Prepare Before Applying

Documentation varies by lender, project, financing amount, age of the business and type of facility. Depending on the application, the lender may ask for:

  • business registration or incorporation records and ownership information;
  • historical and interim financial statements, tax information and existing debt details;
  • a business plan, financial forecasts, monthly cash flow and sources-and-uses schedule;
  • equipment quotations, renovation estimates, purchase agreements and lease documents;
  • acquisition records where a business is being purchased; and
  • management resumés and evidence of the owner's contribution; and
  • a personal financial statement showing the owner's assets, liabilities and other commitments, if requested by the lender.

This is a preparation list, not a universal checklist. Ask the selected lender what it requires before assembling a large submission.

Why the plan still matters when government shares risk

A risk-sharing arrangement does not create customer demand or make repayments affordable. A useful plan connects the proposed asset to operating results: what the equipment will produce, how customers will be reached, what staff and inputs are needed, and when cash will arrive. That gives the lender a business case to assess alongside credit and security. ISED's CSBFP Guidelines, section 1 (checked October 2026), requires lender due diligence including repayment assessment; this document list is practical preparation guidance, not an official universal checklist.

Organize the package so each important assumption has an explanation. A supplier quote supports purchase cost; it does not support forecast sales. A personal financial statement helps describe the owner's position; it does not replace the business cash-flow forecast. Historical results show what happened; the investment case explains why the next period might differ. Distinguishing these roles helps avoid using one document to answer a question it cannot resolve.

Common Problems That Can Weaken a Financing Application

Some applications are difficult to assess because the request, evidence and forecast do not agree. Common examples include:

  • a financing request that does not reconcile to quotations or project costs;
  • revenue assumptions that are not supported by customer volume, pricing or capacity;
  • too little working capital for the startup or expansion period;
  • an owner contribution whose amount or source is unclear;
  • hiring, equipment or opening dates that differ between the plan and forecast;
  • loan payments omitted from cash flow;
  • unexplained negative cash balances;
  • an acquisition price or startup budget without adequate support; and
  • financing requested for costs that may not qualify under the program.

These issues do not automatically cause a rejection. They can, however, make it harder for the lender to understand the transaction or become comfortable with the repayment case.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Canada Small Business Financing Program?

CSBFP is a federal program administered by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada. Participating financial institutions provide the financing and make the credit decisions. The government shares eligible lending risk under the program, but that arrangement does not guarantee approval.

Who qualifies for a CSBFP loan?

ISED’s small-business FAQ includes eligible startups and existing for-profit, not-for-profit and charitable businesses operating in Canada with gross annual revenue of CAD $10 million or less. Farming businesses are excluded. The lender must approve the borrower.

How much can you borrow through CSBFP?

ISED’s Helping small businesses get loans publishes a combined ceiling of CAD $1.15 million: up to CAD $1 million in term loans and CAD $150,000 in lines of credit. Cost-category sub-limits apply; the lender decides the approved amount.

What can a CSBFP loan be used for?

Depending on the facility and current rules, financing may cover eligible real property, equipment, leasehold improvements, intangible assets and working-capital costs. The lender should confirm the treatment of each proposed cost because not every expense in a project budget necessarily qualifies.

Which banks offer CSBFP loans?

Participating banks, credit unions and caisses populaires offer financing under the program. Participation and local lending practices can change. Use ISED's participating-lender directory, then contact the institution to confirm that it handles the type of request you are preparing.

Does the Government of Canada approve a CSBFP loan?

No. The entrepreneur applies through a participating financial institution. That lender reviews the application, performs due diligence, assesses repayment ability and decides whether to lend. Eligible financing can then be registered under the program.

Do I need a business plan for a CSBFP loan?

Not every application follows one universal document list. A lender may request a plan, particularly for a startup, acquisition, major expansion, substantial request or business without a long financial history. Ask the lender what it needs for the particular application.

What documents does a CSBFP lender need?

The lender sets its checklist. It may ask for a business plan, financial projections, a personal financial statement, asset quotes or invoices, business records, historical financials and proof of owner funds. The plan should reconcile the financing request, project costs and repayment forecast. This is preparation guidance, not a universal program requirement.

Can a startup qualify for CSBFP financing?

Yes, an eligible startup may apply. Without historical business results, the lender may place more weight on owner experience, credit, investment, quotations, market evidence and detailed cash-flow assumptions. Startup status does not remove the lender's normal credit review.

Does CSBFP guarantee loan approval?

No. Program eligibility and lender approval are separate matters. The financial institution decides whether the borrower and business can support the proposed financing and may decline an application even when the business or costs appear eligible.

What is the difference between CSBFP and CSBFA?

CSBFP means the Canada Small Business Financing Program. CSBFA means the Canada Small Business Financing Act, the legislation that provides the framework for the program. They are related terms, not two separate loan programs.

What happens if a business defaults on a CSBFP loan?

The borrower remains responsible for repayment. Government loss-sharing protects eligible lender losses, not the borrower from collection. The lender may enforce security and any applicable guarantees under the signed agreements and law. Contact the lender early if payments become difficult and seek independent advice about your obligations.
