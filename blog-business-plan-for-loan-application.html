Business Plan for a Loan Application: What Canadian Lenders Actually Check

Published June 21, 2023

Cash flow, owner finances, security and evidence behind your funding request.

  1. Home
  2. Learning Centre
  3. Loan application business plan

A business plan for a loan application answers a different question from an investor pitch: how will the money be repaid? Growth potential matters, but a lender also needs to understand cash availability, existing obligations and what happens if trading is weaker than expected. For Canadian owners preparing a small business loan request, the strongest starting point is a consistent explanation of the funding need, supported by evidence and connected financial projections.

The five things lenders check

1. Cash flow and debt-service capacity

Profit and cash are different. A profitable business can run short of money while waiting for customers to pay, buying inventory or covering seasonal costs. Lenders examine whether cash generated by the business can support existing commitments and the proposed borrowing. Debt service includes principal and interest, so an income statement alone does not tell the whole repayment story.

Good preparation means showing when cash actually arrives and leaves. Connect projected sales to collection terms, supplier payments, payroll, rent, taxes and inventory purchases. Include existing loan payments and proposed repayments using clearly identified financing assumptions. Explain owner compensation or withdrawals consistently with the business structure, so personal living needs do not disappear from the forecast.

A lender may use debt-service ratios, but definitions and acceptable results depend on the lender, product and circumstances. Rather than relying on a supposed universal pass mark, make the calculation understandable and test a slower-sales or delayed-collections case. Our guide to business loans in Canada provides context for comparing borrowing routes before preparing the application.

2. The owner’s track record and personal financial position

A lender needs to understand who will execute the plan. Relevant industry experience, management responsibilities and a record of handling business finances help explain the owner's ability to operate the company. For an established business, financial statements and recent operating results provide evidence. For a startup, experience, preparation and evidence of demand carry more of the explanation.

Personal financial information may also be requested, particularly where an owner provides a guarantee. A lender may assess personal credit, assets, liabilities and other obligations alongside the business application. The exact requests depend on the financing arrangement; a business plan does not replace credit assessment.

Good looks like a concise management profile supported by relevant experience, clear ownership information and accurate financial disclosures. Explain past difficulties factually and describe what has changed. Identify gaps in the team and how they will be addressed. If the plan includes an owner contribution, show its source and availability rather than treating it as money that will somehow appear.

3. Collateral and security

Collateral gives a lender an interest in specified assets under the financing agreement. Depending on the transaction, security may relate to equipment, property or business assets. A personal guarantee is a separate commitment by a guarantor; it should not be confused with pledging a particular asset. Requirements vary with the lender, program, product and risk assessment.

A useful plan lists the assets being acquired, who owns them and any existing financing or claims against them. Support purchase costs with documentation, and distinguish cost from estimated resale value. A lender may seek valuations or other security documentation. Do not assume that an asset's purchase price is the amount a lender will recognise as security.

Collateral does not remove the need to explain repayment from operations. If you lack substantial assets, discuss suitable products with the lender before building the entire financing strategy around an assumed unsecured approval.

4. A specific, costed and eligible use of funds

“Working capital and growth” is too broad to explain a funding request. Break the project into identifiable costs such as equipment, leasehold improvements, inventory or operating cash needs. State what each item enables, when payment is due and which source of money will cover it.

Good looks like a sources-and-uses schedule that balances: project costs equal proposed borrowing plus documented owner funds and any other confirmed financing. Attach quotes or estimates, identify applicable taxes and installation costs, and explain allowances for costs that remain uncertain. Distinguish confirmed funding from applications still awaiting a decision.

Eligibility is a separate question from affordability. A cost can be commercially sensible without qualifying under a particular loan product or program. If you are considering the Canada Small Business Financing Program, check the current eligible costs and documentation requirements with the participating lender. Program eligibility does not replace the lender's credit decision.

5. Realistic assumptions behind the projections

A spreadsheet is only as credible as the reasoning behind it. Explain sales through practical drivers: customers served, units sold, prices, capacity and the time needed to win business. Connect staffing, materials and other expenses to that activity. Distinguish signed orders from prospects and general market interest.

Good looks like an assumptions page with sources and dates. Existing operators can use historical trading patterns and explain departures from them. Startups can support assumptions with supplier quotes, customer research, relevant experience and realistic operating constraints. Broad market growth does not, by itself, prove that your business will gain customers.

Show what happens if opening is delayed, sales grow more slowly or key costs increase. Explain the operational response and any resulting funding gap. A downside scenario should reveal the pressure points and possible actions, rather than simply lowering revenue while leaving every other number unchanged.

The document package: a preparation checklist

Ask the intended lender for its document list before submitting. Requirements differ, but the following checklist helps organise a coherent package:

  • Business plan: business model, market evidence, management, operating plan, financing request, use of funds and repayment explanation.
  • Financial projections: connected income statements, cash flow forecasts and balance sheets, with assumptions, existing debt and a downside case.
  • Historical business records, where available: financial statements, recent interim results, bank statements and tax documents requested by the lender.
  • Personal statements: requested statements of assets and liabilities, details of obligations and evidence supporting the owner's proposed contribution.
  • Quotes and estimates: supplier proposals, equipment details, renovation estimates and relevant lease or purchase documents.
  • Business and ownership documents: registration or incorporation records, ownership details, relevant permits and material contracts, as requested.

Use consistent figures and dates throughout. If a supplier quote changes, update the funding request and projections together. Label estimates, keep a record of revisions and explain missing documents rather than allowing an incomplete attachment to create confusion.

How banks, BDC, credit unions and alternative lenders read it

The repayment question remains central across lender types. Differences arise from the specific product, mandate, underwriting approach and available evidence. Avoid assuming that one category always requires less documentation or accepts weaker cash flow.

Banks: expect the plan to fit the requested facility. Financing an asset purchase raises different questions from arranging operating credit. Established results, account activity, existing debts and security may all inform the assessment. Explain why the borrowing structure matches the asset or cash cycle being financed.

BDC: Canada's business development bank has a mandate to support Canadian entrepreneurs. An application still needs a credible business case, financial information and a repayment explanation. Requirements depend on the financing offered and the applicant's circumstances; its development role is not a promise of approval.

Credit unions: these are member-owned financial institutions. Membership and service areas can affect where an owner applies. Provide the same clear evidence of business viability and repayment, and ask the individual credit union about its products and documentation. Local knowledge does not remove credit assessment.

Alternative lenders: this category covers different providers and products, so there is no single reading method. Depending on the product, a provider may focus on recent revenue, bank transactions, receivables or asset values. Understand the total borrowing cost, payment frequency, security and guarantee terms, then model their effect on cash. A shorter application does not make the repayment obligation smaller.

Red flags that slow an application

Unexplained round-number budgets: rounded estimates can be reasonable early in planning, but an unsupported lump sum leaves the reviewer guessing. Replace it with itemised costs and evidence, clearly identifying anything still provisional.

No assumptions page: a revenue forecast without prices, volumes, timing or sources cannot be checked easily. Show how the numbers were built and who is responsible for achieving the operating targets.

Seasonality or owner pay omitted: equal sales every month can hide a winter slowdown or a peak-season inventory purchase. Likewise, leaving out the owner's salary or withdrawals can overstate available cash. Show these items explicitly and explain how quieter periods will be funded.

Conflicting documents: different loan amounts across the plan, application and spreadsheet invite follow-up questions. Reconcile the package before submission and identify unresolved financing terms as assumptions.

Frequently asked questions

How far ahead should projections go?

Ask the lender what forecast horizon and detail it requires. Monthly projections are useful for exposing cash timing, seasonality and startup pressures; annual summaries can explain the longer-term direction. The forecast should cover the relevant operating cycle and planned changes, with clearly stated assumptions. There is no single horizon that satisfies every lender or financing request.

Do I need collateral for a small business loan?

It depends on the lender, product, program and application. Some financing is secured; other products may be unsecured but still involve a personal guarantee or other conditions. Ask what security is required and understand the documents before agreeing. Do not assume that program participation automatically removes security requirements.

Can a startup get a loan with a business plan alone?

A plan alone generally does not establish everything needed for a credit decision. A startup may also need projections, owner financial information, evidence of investment, quotes and support for its assumptions. The lender decides whether the complete application meets its requirements. A well-written plan cannot substitute for a viable repayment case.

How long does approval take?

It varies with the lender, financing type, complexity, document completeness and any security or verification work. Ask about the process for your application and which outstanding items could delay a decision or funding. Approval and release of funds may involve separate conditions. Avoid committing to purchases on the assumption that financing will arrive by a particular date.
