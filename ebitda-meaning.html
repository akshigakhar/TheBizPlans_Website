EBITDA Meaning: What It Is and Why Lenders Care

A plain-English guide to the formula, the arithmetic and the questions behind the number.

EBITDA meaning: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization — a measure of a company's operating earning power with financing costs, tax jurisdiction, and non-cash accounting charges stripped out. If you've ever sat across from a lender who asked about your EBITDA and nodded along without fully knowing what they meant, this guide fixes that in ten minutes.

You'll get the plain-English definition, the formula, a worked example with real arithmetic, how EBITDA differs from profit and cash flow, why banks care about it, and where it shows up in your business plan.

EBITDA meaning in plain English

EBITDA is an attempt to answer one question: how much does the core business earn from its operations, before financing decisions, tax situations, and accounting write-downs muddy the picture?

Two companies can run identical operations and report very different net incomes — one carries debt (interest expense), one bought equipment years ago (depreciation), they sit in different tax situations. EBITDA strips those differences away so you can compare the operating engines side by side.

The intuition: if you want to know whether the business itself works, look at EBITDA. If you want to know whether the owners get paid and the loans get serviced, keep reading down the income statement.

What does EBITDA stand for?

  • E — Earnings: start from net income (the bottom line).
  • B — Before: each item below gets added back.
  • I — Interest: the cost of debt. Added back because financing choices shouldn't distort operating performance.
  • T — Taxes: income tax expense. Added back because tax situations vary by jurisdiction and structure.
  • D — Depreciation: the non-cash write-down of tangible assets (equipment, vehicles). Added back because no cash leaves the building.
  • A — Amortization: the same idea for intangible assets (software, patents, goodwill).

The EBITDA formula

EBITDA = Net Income + Interest + Taxes + Depreciation + Amortization

That's it. Take the bottom line and add back the four items. The result is always higher than net income — which is exactly why you should understand what was added back before you celebrate the number.

Worked example: calculating EBITDA

A fictional Toronto services company reports the following (all figures illustrative, in $ CAD):

Line itemIllustrative amount
Revenue$500,000
Operating expenses($365,000)
Operating income$135,000
Interest expense($10,000)
Depreciation & amortization($25,000)
Income before tax$100,000
Income tax (illustrative)($40,000)
Net income$60,000

Now add back the four items:

Add-backIllustrative amount
Net income$60,000
+ Interest$10,000
+ Taxes$40,000
+ Depreciation & amortization$25,000
= EBITDA$135,000

EBITDA margin = $135,000 ÷ $500,000 = 27% (illustrative).

Read: the business earns $135,000 from operations on $500,000 of revenue — but $75,000 of that is consumed by debt service, taxes, and the wearing-out of assets before the owners see a dollar. Both numbers are true; they just answer different questions.

Why this matters: a company can have glowing EBITDA and shrinking net income — usually because debt or asset write-downs are eating the difference. If someone pitches you on EBITDA alone, ask what was added back and why.

EBITDA vs. cash flow

This is the confusion that costs founders the most. EBITDA is not cash flow. It ignores:

  • Capital expenditures — the new equipment you must buy to keep operating.
  • Working capital — the receivables and inventory your cash is trapped in.
  • Debt principal repayments — interest is added back, but the principal still has to be paid.

A business can post healthy EBITDA while bleeding cash — growing receivables, heavy reinvestment needs, big principal payments. Banks know this, which is why they underwrite cash flow, not EBITDA alone. (How banks actually read your statements: how banks evaluate business plans.)

Why lenders and investors care about EBITDA

Three reasons this number comes up in every financing conversation:

  1. Comparability. A lender comparing two borrowers needs a number that isn't distorted by one company's debt load or another's depreciation schedule. EBITDA is the closest thing to an apples-to-apples operating measure.
  2. Debt-service sanity check. Lenders relate your debt to EBITDA — if the loan payments consume most of it, there's no margin for a bad quarter. This is the quick screen before they dig into your cash-flow statement.
  3. Valuation shorthand. Buyers and investors often value small businesses as a multiple of EBITDA. Understanding your own number means understanding what a buyer sees.

The banker's view: when a lender asks for your EBITDA, they're really asking “does this business generate enough operating earnings to survive its debts?” Have the number ready — and have the cash-flow statement behind it.

What EBITDA doesn't tell you

Honesty section — because a metric this popular gets abused:

  • It ignores the cost of staying in business. Depreciation is “non-cash,” but the equipment still wears out and must be replaced. A company with ancient assets flatters its EBITDA right up until the replacement bill arrives.
  • It ignores working capital. Revenue booked is not cash collected. Fast-growing companies routinely show strong EBITDA and empty bank accounts.
  • It can be gamed. Aggressive capitalization of expenses, one-time gains buried in operating income, add-backs that stretch the definition — EBITDA has no single audited standard the way net income does. Always ask what's inside someone else's number.

The practitioner rule I give every client: EBITDA is the opening of the conversation, never the conclusion. The conclusion lives in the cash-flow statement.

Where EBITDA shows up in your business plan

If you're writing a plan for a bank loan or investor round, EBITDA appears in three places:

  1. Financial projections — show it as a line item with the margin percentage, so the reader doesn't have to compute it.
  2. Funding request — relate the ask to EBITDA. A lender mentally checks: can this business's operating earnings service this debt? Make that math easy.
  3. Use of funds narrative — if the loan buys equipment, say how the equipment grows EBITDA. Connect the money to the earnings.

Get these three right and your plan reads like it was written by someone who's sat on the lender's side of the desk — because the financials were built that way.

Frequently asked questions

What does EBITDA stand for?

Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. It's net income with those four items added back, giving a cleaner view of operating performance. The "meaning" people search for is really the intuition: how much the business earns from operations before financing, tax, and accounting choices.

How do you calculate EBITDA?

Start with net income, then add back interest expense, income tax expense, depreciation, and amortization. Formula: EBITDA = Net Income + Interest + Taxes + Depreciation + Amortization. Most accounting software can produce it, but verify what's inside each add-back before quoting the number.

Is EBITDA the same as profit?

No. EBITDA is always higher than net income because it adds back real costs — interest you pay, taxes you owe, and assets wearing out. It measures operating earning power; profit (net income) measures what owners actually earned. Both matter; they answer different questions.

Why do banks look at EBITDA?

Because it lets them compare borrowers on operating performance without distortion from different debt loads or depreciation schedules, and because it feeds the debt-service sanity check: can this business's operating earnings support the loan payments? It's the quick screen — the cash-flow statement is the deep read.

What is a good EBITDA margin?

It depends entirely on the industry and business model — software margins and construction margins live in different worlds, and anyone quoting a universal "good" number is guessing. What matters for your plan: show your margin, show the trend over three years, and be ready to explain what's driving it. An honest, explainable margin beats an impressive, unexplained one.

What's the difference between EBITDA and EBIT?

EBIT (operating income) adds back only interest and taxes — it still deducts depreciation and amortization. EBITDA goes one step further and adds those back too. EBIT is closer to accounting reality; EBITDA is closer to cash operating performance. Lenders use both; know which one you're quoting.
