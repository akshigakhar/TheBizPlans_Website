EBITDA Meaning: What It Is and Why Lenders Care

A plain-English guide to the formula, the arithmetic and the questions behind the number.

EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, a measure of earnings that adds those expenses back to net income. It helps explain operating performance before financing costs, income tax expense and the allocation of certain asset costs. It is useful alongside the income statement and cash-flow statement, rather than as a substitute for either.

This guide explains the calculation, how to interpret the result and where it belongs in a Canadian business plan. Return to the Business Finance Glossary for related terms.

What each letter means

The letters describe which expenses are excluded from the earnings measure. “Before” means adding back expenses already deducted when calculating net income; it does not mean the business avoids paying them.

  • E — Earnings: net income is the starting point for the bottom-up calculation.
  • B — Before: the following expenses are removed from that earnings calculation.
  • I — Interest: borrowing costs are added back to help compare businesses with different financing arrangements.
  • T — Taxes: income tax expense is added back because tax circumstances can differ. This does not mean adding back every tax: payroll and property taxes remain operating costs.
  • D — Depreciation: the allocation of tangible asset costs, such as equipment, over their useful lives is added back. It is a non-cash expense in the period recorded, although buying or replacing equipment requires cash.
  • A — Amortisation: the allocation of finite-life intangible asset costs, such as qualifying software, is added back for the same reason. Asset purchases still matter to the business's finances.

The EBITDA formula

EBITDA = Net Income + Interest + Taxes + Depreciation + Amortisation

Use interest expense and income tax expense for the same reporting period as net income. Add back only depreciation and amortisation already deducted in that period, including amounts within cost of sales. Check the notes or accounting records if these charges are grouped with other expenses.

EBITDA = Operating Profit + Depreciation + Amortisation

The operating-profit route works when operating profit includes depreciation and amortisation and excludes interest and income taxes. If net income contains non-operating gains or losses, the two routes may differ. Reconcile those items and explain the definition used instead of treating operating income and EBIT as automatically identical.

Illustrative example: a fictional Canadian business

Illustrative example: Maple Workshop is a fictional small Canadian repair business. All figures below are invented, in Canadian dollars, for one year. They demonstrate arithmetic and are not industry benchmarks or a recommended tax rate. The business has no other income or expenses in this simplified example.

Illustrative annual income statement (CAD)
Line itemAmount
Revenue$500,000
Operating costs excluding depreciation and amortisation($350,000)
Depreciation($20,000)
Amortisation($5,000)
Operating profit$125,000
Interest expense($10,000)
Income before tax$115,000
Income tax expense($25,000)
Net income$90,000

Starting with net income, add back each excluded expense step by step:

  1. Net income plus interest: $90,000 + $10,000 = $100,000.
  2. Add income tax expense: $100,000 + $25,000 = $125,000.
  3. Add depreciation: $125,000 + $20,000 = $145,000.
  4. Add amortisation: $145,000 + $5,000 = $150,000 EBITDA.

The second route gives the same answer: operating profit of $125,000 + depreciation of $20,000 + amortisation of $5,000 = $150,000. Revenue less operating costs excluding those charges also equals $150,000 here. Keeping these routes consistent helps catch double-counting.

EBITDA margin

EBITDA Margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue × 100

Maple Workshop's illustrative margin is $150,000 ÷ $500,000 × 100 = 30%. That means the example reports 30 cents of EBITDA for each dollar of revenue. It does not mean 30 cents is available for owners to withdraw.

A useful margin comparison needs a consistent calculation and comparable businesses. Industry, pricing, labour requirements and business model affect margins. Explain changes over time through the underlying sales and cost assumptions; there is no universal “good” percentage.

Why lenders and buyers care

Lenders may use EBITDA as a starting point when assessing debt-service capacity: whether operating earnings can support interest and principal repayments. They also consider cash flow, existing debt, capital spending, working capital and their own lending criteria. EBITDA itself does not deduct principal repayments or establish that a loan is affordable.

Buyers may use an EBITDA valuation multiple to compare an acquisition with other businesses. Multiples vary by industry and also depend on the business, risk, growth prospects and transaction terms. A multiple applied to EBITDA usually informs enterprise value; debt, cash and transaction adjustments affect the amount attributable to owners. No particular multiple applies universally.

Where EBITDA belongs in a business plan

Put EBITDA and its margin in the financial section, alongside projected income statements, cash-flow forecasts and balance sheets. Show the reconciliation from operating profit or net income so readers can follow the calculation. Keep historical results separate from forecasts and state the assumptions behind changes in revenue and expenses.

Connect the funding request and repayment schedule to the cash-flow forecast. For help presenting connected financial projections, see our business plan writing services. Our financial forecasting guide explains how the financial statements fit together. Ground sales assumptions in the customers you expect to serve using the blog's guide to identifying your target market.

Limits of EBITDA

EBITDA is not a GAAP measure and does not replace financial statements prepared under the applicable accounting framework. Definitions and adjustments can differ, so disclose the calculation. If presenting adjusted EBITDA, identify each additional adjustment separately and explain it rather than hiding it within ordinary EBITDA.

It ignores capital expenditure: a business can report positive EBITDA while needing substantial equipment replacement. It also ignores working-capital movements, such as cash tied up in inventory or unpaid customer invoices. Finally, it excludes income tax expense and does not measure cash taxes paid. Those payments still reduce available cash.

Compare EBITDA with operating cash flow and planned capital spending before drawing conclusions about liquidity. A profitable sale on credit can increase earnings before the customer pays. Conversely, collecting an older invoice can improve cash without increasing the current period's EBITDA. Timing matters.

Frequently asked questions

Is EBITDA the same as profit?

No. Net income deducts interest, income taxes, depreciation and amortisation. EBITDA adds those expenses back. It measures a different level of earnings and does not show cash available to owners.

What is a good EBITDA margin?

It varies by industry and business model. Compare consistently calculated margins with relevant peers and your own history, and explain the sales and cost assumptions behind the result.

Do banks use EBITDA?

Banks may use EBITDA when assessing operating performance and debt-service capacity. They also review cash flow and other information; the definition and requirements depend on the lender and loan.

EBITDA vs cash flow: what is the difference?

EBITDA measures earnings before specified expenses. Cash flow tracks cash movements and reflects timing differences such as collections and supplier payments. EBITDA does not capture capital expenditure, debt principal payments or cash taxes.
