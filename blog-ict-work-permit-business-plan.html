ICT Work Permit Business Plans: What Officers Actually Assess

What IRCC’s published guidance asks the Canadian operation and transferee to demonstrate.

  1. Home
  2. Learning Centre
  3. ICT work permit business plans

Official IRCC guidance checked 29 October 2026. General information, not immigration advice. Planning suggestions below are distinguished from published requirements; no business plan guarantees a work permit.

What an ICT work permit is

An intra-company transfer (ICT) allows a qualifying multinational enterprise to transfer an existing employee to its Canadian operation under the International Mobility Program. Typical applicants are executives, managers and employees with specialised knowledge. This article covers IRCC’s general provisions under paragraph R205(a), using codes C61, C62 and C63. Transfers under international trade agreements have separate instructions; the applicable category matters.

For a new Canadian enterprise under C61, an established foreign company alone is insufficient. IRCC’s published guidance requires an existing multinational with revenue-generating business operations in at least two countries before establishing the Canadian enterprise. Canada cannot be the company’s first foreign operation through this category. Confirm the applicable route before commissioning a plan.

The business-planning task is to describe a specific Canadian expansion, connect it to the foreign enterprise and explain the employee’s work. It is not enough to describe an attractive industry or the applicant’s ambition to become a Canadian entrepreneur.

Why the business plan is central

For employees establishing a qualifying enterprise, IRCC expressly asks for a business plan and financial documentation showing the foreign enterprise can fund establishment and the initial ramp-up. It also asks for reasonable human-resource plans and evidence of when the enterprise will become actively engaged. These requirements appear in the guidance’s section on establishing a branch, subsidiary or affiliate.

A useful plan brings those elements together: who owns the operation, where work happens, who performs it and how bills get paid. It should reference supporting records rather than replace them with assertions. Incorporation establishes legal existence; it does not, by itself, explain the operation. What officers assess under the published guidance

The six topics below follow IRCC’s published assessment instructions. Suggested exhibits are practical ways to organise evidence, not a universal document checklist. The application category and the enterprise’s stage determine what is relevant.

1. The qualifying relationship

Published basis: The “Qualifying relationship” section addresses parent, branch, subsidiary and affiliate relationships through ownership and control. A customer contract or shared brand does not establish that relationship.

Evidence in the plan: Include an ownership diagram with legal names, jurisdictions, ownership percentages and reporting relationships. Cross-reference incorporation records, share registers and relevant agreements. Explain who directs the Canadian operation and distinguish ownership from commercial cooperation. If the group has restructured, make the current structure clear rather than copying an outdated organisation chart.

2. Commercial premises and an operating footprint

Published basis: IRCC’s “Commercial physical premises” section requires a physical commercial workplace and excludes residential or virtual mailing-address operations. For C61 setup, it recognises initial use of counsel’s address while premises are secured. Shared space is assessed for a legitimate business presence.

Evidence in the plan: Describe the location, intended use, capacity and acquisition or leasing stage. Reference a lease, proposed terms or other available records, clearly identifying unsigned arrangements. Explain how employees and clients use the space. Budget rent, deposits, fit-out and equipment from the same premises assumptions. A registration address should not be presented as an operating facility.

3. Staffing and the transferee’s place in the team

Published basis: C61 instructions ask for reasonable plans to maintain or hire staff and an organisation large enough to support the proposed executive, management or specialised-knowledge function throughout the permit.

Evidence in the plan: Show the transferee’s duties, reporting line and authority alongside existing positions and planned Canadian hires. A hiring schedule can identify each role’s start month, responsibilities and compensation. Explain who sells, delivers, administers and supervises the work. These details test whether the proposed role fits the operation; the cited guidance does not prescribe a universal number of Canadian hires.

4. Financial capacity to establish and sustain operations

Published basis: C61 instructions address the foreign enterprise’s financial ability to establish the Canadian enterprise and fund its ramp-up. The occupational-capacity section also considers investment commitment and continuing foreign-operation viability.

Evidence in the plan: Connect Canadian expenses and wage commitments to identified funding sources. Explain the amount, timing and form of proposed intercompany funding, and reference financial records supporting the foreign enterprise’s capacity. Distinguish money already available from financing still being sought. A projected profit does not show how payroll is funded before customers pay.

5. The executive, managerial or specialised-knowledge basis

Published basis: The C62 section assesses actual executive or managerial functions and whether the Canadian organisation justifies them. Under C63, IRCC requires both advanced proprietary knowledge and advanced expertise; general industry competence alone is insufficient.

Evidence in the plan: Connect the Canadian assignment to the employee’s work abroad, authority and company-specific experience. Reference job descriptions, employment records, training and project evidence as relevant. For a manager, explain who performs the operational work. For a specialist, identify the proprietary systems or processes and why this employee’s expertise matters. A senior title or technical résumé cannot answer every part of that question.

6. A supported timeline to operations

Published basis: IRCC’s C61 “Assessing whether a new enterprise is actively engaged” section explicitly asks for a timeline and supporting evidence. Its extension instructions expect the operation to become actively engaged during the initial year.

Evidence in the plan: Link premises, equipment, any necessary permissions, recruitment, customer development and service delivery in a dated schedule. Identify responsibilities and dependencies. A proposed opening month should agree with payroll, rent and revenue forecasts. Explain what changes financially if setup takes longer. These are business milestones, not promises about IRCC processing or a permit decision.

The financial section: make the numbers traceable

Preparing 12–24 months of operating projections is a practical planning choice, not a forecast horizon prescribed in the cited ICT instructions. Use monthly cash flow where it helps explain establishment and early operations. A longer view may be useful for the particular business, but length alone does not make a model credible.

Start with a sources-and-uses schedule. Separate one-time setup costs from recurring expenses: deposits, fit-out, equipment, initial inventory, professional services and working capital serve different purposes. Attach dates and evidence to material estimates. A supplier quotation and a provisional allowance should be labelled differently so the reader can judge uncertainty.

Build wage commitments from the staffing schedule. State start dates, salary or hourly assumptions, hours and employer costs. Include the transferee and planned hires, and reconcile the total to operating expenses. IRCC’s C63 guidance includes a prevailing-wage floor for the occupation and region; use the applicable current wage information rather than inventing a standard ICT salary.

Forecast sales from observable drivers: customers multiplied by average purchases, billable hours multiplied by rates, or units multiplied by price. Test delivery capacity against the people and equipment budgeted. Record payment terms and collection delays. Revenue in the income statement and cash received in the bank may occur in different months.

Then show opening cash, funding receipts, operating payments and closing cash. Explain the lowest cash balance and how any shortfall would be covered. Avoid treating an intercompany transfer as sales revenue. Use consistent currencies, and explain exchange-rate assumptions where the foreign enterprise funds Canadian-dollar costs.

A slower-sales or delayed-opening scenario helps management see whether the funding commitment still covers rent and wages. For illustration, moving an employee’s start date forward also moves payroll payments forward; the narrative and model must both change. This is a consistency check, not an IRCC formula. The cited instructions do not set a universal minimum investment or required bank balance.

Common planning weaknesses

These are practical weaknesses in presenting evidence, not a published ranking of refusal reasons:

  • Generic template content: Broad market statistics never explain this group’s ownership, Canadian customers or operating model. Replace reusable claims with business-specific evidence.
  • No premises explanation: A mailing address leaves the commercial workplace unresolved. Describe the actual setup stage and supporting records.
  • Staffing disconnected from sales: A small delivery team cannot automatically support unlimited growth. Match hiring dates and productive capacity to forecast work.
  • Projections without assumptions: Smooth percentage growth hides pricing, customer volumes, payment delays and funding needs. Make the calculation visible.

Before finalising, compare the plan, organisation chart, employment description, premises records and model. Flag contradictions and unresolved facts for the applicant and representative. A polished document remains weak evidence if its underlying records tell a different story.

Frequently asked questions

How long is an ICT work permit issued for?

IRCC publishes a maximum initial C61 duration of one year, and initial C62 and C63 maxima of three years. C61 extensions are exceptional; the specific extension section describes up to six additional months for qualifying delays beyond the applicant’s or employer’s control. Actual validity depends on the category, offer and travel-document limits. These are permit-validity rules, not processing times or guaranteed durations.

Does the Canadian company need revenue already?

IRCC distinguishes an existing operation from a new C61 enterprise being established. C61 asks for a supported timeline to active business, so existing Canadian revenue is not a blanket prerequisite for every setup application. Incorporation alone is insufficient, and the foreign multinational must meet the prior revenue-generating operations requirement. Explain when Canadian activity begins and how its ramp-up is funded.

What is the difference between ICT and L1?

L1 is the U.S. counterpart for intra-company transfers; see our L1 visa business-plan guide for U.S. planning context, with eligibility assessed separately by U.S. counsel.

Who writes the plan: lawyer, consultant or applicant?

The cited ICT guidance requires evidence and a plan for C61; it does not designate a mandatory author. The applicant and company provide the facts, a planning specialist can develop the commercial narrative and model, and an authorised immigration representative can assess application strategy.
