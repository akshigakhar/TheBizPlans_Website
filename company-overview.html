Company Overview: What It Is and How to Write One

A practical checklist, writing steps and an original fictional illustration.

A company overview is the section of a business plan, proposal or profile that summarises what a business is, what it does and how it is set up. It introduces the company's identity, ownership, offer, customers, operations and direction so readers can understand the organisation before assessing the detailed plan.

Think of it as the factual introduction to the business. A reader should be able to identify who operates it, what customers buy and whether it is trading or preparing to launch. The overview provides context for the market analysis, operating plan and financial projections that follow.

This guide explains what to include, how to organise the section and how to distinguish established facts from intentions. For related definitions, visit the business finance glossary.

What does a company overview include?

Use this checklist to gather the essentials. Not every item needs a separate heading: combine related facts into short paragraphs and give more space to details that explain your particular business.

  • ☐ Legal structure and ownership: Give the registered name, any trading name, jurisdiction and structure, such as a sole proprietorship, partnership or corporation. Identify the owners and their responsibilities; distinguish the operating company from a parent or related entity.
  • ☐ History and founding: State when and why the business began and its current stage. Include changes in ownership or direction when they explain today's operation, rather than recounting every event since incorporation.
  • ☐ Products and services: Describe what customers purchase, the problem it addresses and how the business earns revenue. Use familiar words before introducing technical terms, product names or specialist processes.
  • ☐ Target market: Identify the customer group, need and geography in one line; reserve calculations for the TAM, SAM and SOM market-sizing analysis.
  • ☐ Location and operations: Explain where the business works and how it delivers its offer. Mention premises, online channels, service territory and material supplier arrangements; distinguish secured facilities from proposed ones.
  • ☐ Team: Name the people responsible for management and delivery, with relevant experience. Identify roles still to be filled instead of presenting planned hires as existing staff. Leave full biographies for the management section.
  • ☐ Mission and vision: Explain the purpose the business serves today and its longer-term direction. Connect both to practical choices about customers, products or delivery rather than relying on a slogan.
  • ☐ Milestones and traction to date: Include completed work that establishes the current stage, such as a prototype, signed lease or completed pilot. State dates and keep supporting records available. Describe customer interest accurately without implying it is contracted revenue.
  • ☐ Goals: Set out the next priorities with timing and a responsible person or role. Make clear which goals depend on financing, permissions or recruitment. Goals describe intended actions, not promised results.

Company overview vs executive summary

The overview introduces the organisation. The executive summary brings together the whole plan's case, including the proposed decision and relevant financial information. Some facts overlap, but the sections serve different reading needs.

Keep the overview focused even when the business has several divisions. Explain the entity covered by this plan, then direct readers to supporting detail. The summary can draw on those facts without repeating the entire company history.

How to write a company overview

  1. Identify the reader and instructions. Decide whether you are introducing a borrower, investment opportunity or supplier.

    Tip: Check the recipient's headings and length limit before drafting; a proposal may prescribe the information and order.

  2. Collect the current facts. Confirm legal identity, ownership, management, location and operating status against your records.

    Tip: Mark anything unconfirmed so a draft assumption cannot quietly become a statement of fact.

  3. Write the opening description. Explain what the business sells, to whom and where, then identify its structure and stage.

    Tip: Read it aloud to someone outside your industry and replace any phrase they cannot explain.

  4. Add the relevant background. Select the founding reason, team capabilities and completed milestones that explain the operation today.

    Tip: Retain a historical detail only when it helps the reader understand a present capability or constraint.

  5. Connect purpose to next steps. Describe the mission, vision and immediate goals, keeping future plans separate from completed work.

    Tip: Use explicit status words such as “operates”, “is developing” and “plans to lease”.

  6. Reconcile and edit. Compare names, dates, staffing and locations with the rest of the plan, then remove repetition.

    Tip: Ask whether a reader could explain the business after one reading without needing the sales pitch.

Fictional example: annotated company overview

The paragraph below is an original fictional illustration. The company, people and activities are invented; the bracketed notes identify the checklist items each sentence illustrates.

Birch Lantern Repairs Inc. is a fictional Ontario corporation founded by its owner, Mira Chen, to repair household lamps in Kingston. [Legal structure, ownership and founding] It offers workshop repairs and local collection for residents who want to keep existing lighting in use, operating from a leased workshop managed by Mira. [Products, target market, location and team] Its mission is to make repair practical, with a longer-term vision of a neighbourhood repair workshop; its service intake process has been drafted. [Mission, vision and milestone to date] Its next goal is to appoint a repair technician before introducing scheduled collection days. [Goals]

This compact paragraph demonstrates the structure rather than providing a complete business plan section. A real overview would add verified dates, relevant experience and operational detail. Remove the annotations in your finished document and retain records supporting any stated milestones.

Common mistakes

  • Copying the mission statement verbatim: A slogan leaves basic questions unanswered. Explain what the purpose means for the offer and customer, then provide the practical company facts.
  • All history, no market: A detailed founding story does not identify today's buyer. Cut background that does not explain the current operation and state who the business serves.
  • Unsupported claims: Replace “leading”, “unique” or “guaranteed” with specific, verifiable descriptions. Separate completed milestones from plans and avoid treating expressions of interest as sales.

Frequently asked questions

How long is a company overview?

As a drafting guide, aim for roughly half a page to two pages. A simple startup may need only a few paragraphs; a more complex company may need subheadings. Follow any recipient limit and move detailed evidence to the appendix.

Where does it go in a business plan?

Usually place it after the executive summary and before the market analysis. It may be called the company description or business overview. Follow the required plan structure when a lender, investor or programme supplies one.

Company overview vs company profile — same thing?

The terms can overlap. An overview usually introduces the business within a larger document; a company profile may stand alone as a supplier or marketing introduction. Check the requested content rather than assuming either label defines a fixed format.

What tense and format should I use?

Use present tense for current facts, past tense for completed events and future wording for plans. Write short paragraphs with descriptive headings; use bullets for goals or milestones. Keep names, dates and terminology consistent with the rest of the document.
