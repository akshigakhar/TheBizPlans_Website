L-1A and L-1B: two tracks, different business explanations

USCIS identifies L-1A as the intracompany-transfer classification for executives and managers, and L-1B as the classification for employees with specialized knowledge. Both concern transfers within a qualifying organization. Source: USCIS, L-1 Intracompany Transferees policy alert. In practical drafting terms, an L-1A plan explains the proposed leadership role and the organization supporting it; an L-1B plan explains the operation’s need for the employee’s particular company knowledge.

For L-1A, describe decisions, reporting relationships and responsibility for the business or function rather than relying on a senior title. USCIS’s evidence guidance evaluates duties in the context of the personnel structure and the nature and scope of the business. For L-1B, it asks for detailed services, the nature of the specialized knowledge and evidence of the employee’s relevant training and experience. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 2, Part L, Chapter 8, sections A and C.

A useful plan translates that distinction into understandable operations. Who decides which customers to pursue? Who performs delivery work? Which systems or processes does the transferred employee know, and how will that knowledge be used? We document the client’s answers and supporting records. Your U.S. immigration attorney determines the appropriate classification and whether the evidence meets its legal standard.

The qualifying relationship: parent, branch, subsidiary or affiliate

USCIS requires evidence that the petitioner is a qualifying organization, including a parent, branch, affiliate or subsidiary relationship. Its documentation guidance also addresses business activity in the United States and another country. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section A. The relationship is a corporate connection to document, not simply a shared brand or a commercial partnership.

USCIS explains that the foreign and U.S. employers may be the same employer through branch offices, related as parent and subsidiary, or related as affiliates; ownership and control are central to that analysis. Source: USCIS Administrative Appeals Office, July 9, 2024 decision, qualifying-relationship discussion. This is a non-precedent decision illustrating the agency’s analysis, not a separate rule. We map the entities, ownership records and reporting lines; counsel confirms the legal relationship.

The new-office plan: connect requirements to evidence

The new-office case is the centrepiece of our L1 business plan service. USCIS’s L-1A new-office guidance requires the intended operation to support an executive or managerial position within one year of petition approval. It calls for information about the proposed office’s scope, organizational structure and financial goals, the U.S. investment and foreign entity’s financial ability to remunerate the beneficiary, and the foreign entity’s structure. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section B (checked October 26, 2026).

The plan is an organized explanation of that commercial evidence. It should let a reader move from a statement in the narrative to the underlying document and then to the related forecast assumption. A projected launch date, for example, needs to agree with premises availability, equipment delivery, recruitment and the start of revenue. An attractive narrative cannot resolve contradictory dates or missing client records.

Premises secured and suited to the proposed activity

USCIS requires evidence that sufficient physical premises have been secured for a new office. It states this for both L-1A and L-1B new-office petitions. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section B. Our preparation work explains the actual location, available space, intended use, occupancy timing and associated operating costs using documents supplied by the client.

We ask how the proposed premises support the business model: customer meetings, storage, production, administration or service delivery. Lease terms and occupancy assumptions should reconcile with the forecast. If space is still under discussion, the draft records that status accurately and flags the gap to counsel. We do not describe an unsigned proposal as secured premises or assume that any address satisfies the legal requirement.

The beneficiary’s qualifying employment abroad

The one-year employment rule also needs documentary attention. USCIS’s new-office regulation, reproduced in its agency decision, requires one continuous year of executive or managerial employment in the three-year period preceding filing for an L-1A new-office beneficiary. Its Policy Manual confirms that specialized-knowledge experience alone does not establish the qualifying foreign experience for that L-1A new-office case. Sources: USCIS AAO, June 19, 2015 decision, quotation of 8 CFR 214.2(l)(3)(v)(B); USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section B (checked October 26, 2026).

We build an employment chronology from client records: employer name, dates, positions, reporting lines and actual responsibilities. Appointment letters, payroll records and organization materials should tell a consistent story. Counsel checks the relevant dates and any travel or status complications. The plan writer’s role is to make the documented history clear, not to determine eligibility from a résumé alone.

L-1B new-office emphasis

For an L-1B new office, USCIS calls for specialized knowledge, sufficient secured premises and financial ability to remunerate the beneficiary and commence U.S. business. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section B. We therefore explain the employee’s contribution to delivery, implementation or company processes, supported by training and work records. The L-1A one-year managerial-position analysis should not be copied into an L-1B plan as if the two tracks were identical.

First-year staffing ramp and organizational charts

For the L-1A new-office narrative, work backwards from the proposed leadership role at the end of the first year. USCIS recognizes that a manager or executive opening a new office may initially be more involved in daily operations, while requiring authority and intent to hire staff and broad decision-making responsibility. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section B. The commercial question is how the business will move from launch tasks to a supported management structure.

Our staffing schedule connects responsibilities, recruitment stages, operating capacity and payroll costs. It explains who handles sales follow-up, customer delivery, administration and financial records as the operation develops. A proposed hire should have a reason, a funding assumption and work to perform. We distinguish employees from contractors and shared foreign-team support, with counsel reviewing how those arrangements affect the case.

Prepare two clearly dated charts: the current foreign organization and the projected U.S. organization. The first uses actual roles and reporting lines; the second identifies proposed positions and planned hiring stages. Add a launch view and a first-year endpoint where useful. Every projected role remains labelled proposed until the client confirms an actual appointment. Charts should agree with job descriptions and the financial model rather than presenting a larger team than the budget supports.

Illustrative organization example: a foreign leadership team supports a proposed U.S. executive, with proposed sales and delivery functions reporting through the U.S. structure. These are illustrative functions, not a required employee count or an agency-approved arrangement. A role-and-responsibility diagram is more informative than adding impressive titles without explaining who performs the underlying work.

We do not prescribe a universal first-year headcount. USCIS examines actual duties and the organization supporting them; its evidence guidance discusses staffing, operational work and corroborating payroll records. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, section C. The plan should demonstrate the proposed role through credible operations and evidence, rather than treat a made-up number of hires as a compliance shortcut.

U.S. market analysis: turn TAM, SAM and SOM into a sales plan

A Canadian customer base can provide useful experience, but the U.S. operation needs its own market explanation. Define the offer, buyer, geographic reach, competitive alternatives and route to sale. Use dated research that matches the actual product and location. Separate confirmed customer commitments from conversations, expressions of interest and the company’s own estimates. A foreign revenue history should not silently become a U.S. sales forecast.

Use TAM, SAM and SOM to distinguish the broad opportunity from achievable demand. TAM describes the total defined market, SAM narrows it to the customers and territory the offer can serve, and SOM reflects a realistic obtainable share within the plan’s period. The business-plan glossary provides related definitions. These are commercial planning tools, not a claimed USCIS requirement to use particular acronyms.

Build the forecast from reachable customers, acquisition channels, conversion assumptions, order frequency, price and delivery capacity. Record the evidence behind each input and show where an assumption remains uncertain. If the model depends on repeat purchases, explain why customers return. If it depends on a distributor, describe the documented relationship and avoid treating a prospective agreement as completed revenue.

For Canadian expansion clients, we also review differences in pricing, currency, freight, local service capacity and customer expectations. The relevant questions vary by business. A professional-services launch and a goods distributor will have different cash cycles and capacity constraints. The market section should lead directly into operational milestones and the financial model, allowing the reader to trace why the planned staffing and premises make commercial sense.

Three-to-five-year financial projections, labelled illustrative

Our preparation approach uses three-to-five-year projections, with monthly detail for the first year where appropriate. This is a modelling scope, not a universal USCIS forecast-length rule. All prospective financial figures are labelled illustrative projections; historical figures are separately identified as client-supplied actuals. The model normally links an income statement, cash-flow forecast and balance sheet to documented assumptions and supporting schedules.

Start with sources and uses of funds: what is available, what has been spent, what remains committed and what is still proposed. Reconcile premises costs, equipment, launch spending, recruitment, payroll and working capital. The opening balance sheet should connect to the actual transaction records. Revenue should reflect the market analysis and the timing of customer delivery, while cash receipts reflect payment terms rather than assuming every invoice is collected immediately.

Show intercompany support explicitly. Explain which entity pays shared costs, how transfers are recorded and which currency each schedule uses. Obtain appropriate tax and accounting advice on cross-border arrangements. An unexplained cash injection can conceal a funding gap; treating foreign-company cash as automatically available to the U.S. operation can overstate practical resources. We document the client’s funding assumptions and request evidence of the proposed support.

Established-office expansion: begin with actual performance

An operating U.S. business needs a different drafting starting point. USCIS’s non-new-office evidence guidance discusses business activity, annual reports, financial statements, tax returns and the beneficiary’s proposed duties. For an extension after the initial new-office period, it also specifies evidence of continued qualifying organizations, U.S. business activity, duties, staffing and wages where applicable, and financial status. Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, sections A and B.

We organize historical financials, payroll evidence, customer records and the current organization before drafting the growth plan. Separate actual results from forecasts and explain changes from earlier plans. If recruitment was delayed or a customer contract changed, identify the operational and financial consequences. An updated chart should reflect today’s business; future hires belong in a separate projected view. Counsel determines the evidence required for the particular filing.

Canada-to-U.S. expansion: coordinate the commercial story

For a Canadian company opening a U.S. operation, our work connects the Canadian company’s actual resources with the proposed U.S. business. We review the product offering, management responsibilities, financial capacity and operational support across both entities. The aim is a consistent commercial narrative that makes clear what remains in Canada, what is being built in the United States and how each activity is funded.

The Canadian side has its own context. See the immigration hub’s C11 and ICT planning card, the Canadian ICT business-plan resource and the C11 / owner-operator resource. These Canadian resources are separate from the U.S. L-1 analysis. Reusing financial records or research does not make Canadian and U.S. programs interchangeable; each file needs its own professional review and accurate jurisdiction-specific explanation.

How The Biz Plans prepares your L1 visa business plan

  1. Scope the assignment. Share the proposed U.S. activity, business stage, available records and attorney’s instructions. We agree on research, modelling, charts and review responsibilities before drafting.
  2. Build the evidence inventory. Organize corporate records, employment history, premises documents, actual financials and client-supplied commercial materials. Record gaps and distinguish completed actions from proposals.
  3. Develop the operating plan and model. Connect U.S. market evidence to sales, delivery capacity, staffing, funding and cash flow. Review whether the narrative and illustrative projections agree.
  4. Review with the client and counsel. Confirm factual accuracy, resolve conflicting dates and align the commercial explanation with counsel’s legal instructions. The client verifies business facts; the attorney handles legal assessment and filing.
  5. Finalize the agreed deliverables. Provide the business narrative, supporting schedules and financial projections within the engagement scope, with assumptions, sources and proposed actions clearly identified.

Frequently asked questions

Does every L-1 petition need a business plan?

No. USCIS does not mandate a standalone business-plan document in all L-1 cases. Its Policy Manual describes evidence of eligibility and allows different supporting documents. In a new-office case, a business plan organizes the evidence about the proposed operation, funding, staffing and role; it does not replace underlying records. Ask your licensed U.S. immigration attorney what the particular petition requires.

Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, sections A–C (checked October 26, 2026).

What must an L-1 new-office plan include?

For L-1A, address secured sufficient physical premises, qualifying executive or managerial employment abroad, and how the U.S. operation will support an executive or managerial position within one year of approval. Explain the office’s scope, organizational structure, financial goals, investment and financial support. Our plan also connects market research, staffing and illustrative projections to the records. L-1B new-office requirements differ and include specialized knowledge, secured premises and financial ability to pay the beneficiary and commence business.

Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, sections A–C (checked October 26, 2026).

How many employees does the U.S. office need in year one?

Do not work backwards from an invented headcount requirement. USCIS evaluates the duties and organizational context, including who performs operational work and the staffing evidence supporting the role. For an L-1A new office, explain how the operation will support the proposed executive or managerial position within one year of approval. The appropriate structure depends on the business and evidence; counsel should review it.

Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, sections A–C (checked October 26, 2026).

L-1A vs. L-1B — does the plan differ?

Yes. USCIS identifies L-1A with executives and managers and L-1B with specialized-knowledge employees. An L-1A plan explains the leadership duties and supporting organization; an L-1B plan explains the particular knowledge, intended services and business need. USCIS lists separate new-office considerations for each. The L-1A managerial-position analysis should not simply be copied into an L-1B plan.

Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, sections A–C; USCIS, L-1 Intracompany Transferees policy alert (checked October 26, 2026).

Can a Canadian company use an L-1 to open its first U.S. office?

Potentially, if the company and beneficiary satisfy the applicable U.S. requirements. USCIS describes L classification as enabling a foreign company without an affiliated U.S. office to send an executive or manager to establish one. A Canadian company’s location alone does not establish eligibility. Have a licensed U.S. immigration attorney assess the corporate relationship, employment history, proposed role and new-office evidence. Canadian C11 and ICT planning is separate.

Source: USCIS Policy Manual, Chapter 8, sections A–C; USCIS, Handbook for Employers, section 7.9.2 (checked October 26, 2026).
