Marketing Plan Examples for a Business Plan

Published November 12, 2026

Turn customer research into a practical acquisition plan with owners, budgets and measurable assumptions.

Article Overview

A marketing plan explains who a business will reach, what it will offer, which channels it will use and how spending will lead to measurable sales. In a business plan, it should connect customer research to the revenue forecast, staffing and cash budget.

The three fictional examples below show that connection across different industries. All budgets, targets and conversion assumptions are illustrative monthly figures in a single chosen currency, USD or CAD. They are not benchmarks, observed results or recommended spending levels.

Start with a reachable customer

Define the service area, purchase occasion and decision-maker before choosing channels. Use the target-market guide to narrow the audience. A large population does not establish demand; interviews, enquiries and small tests help establish whether a channel can reach likely buyers.

Marketing plan examples by industry

Coffee shop — Corner Kettle

Customer and offer: Nearby weekday commuters seeking a quick breakfast and coffee. The offer is a limited breakfast menu with a clear pickup process.

Channels, budget and owner: The owner will maintain local search information, distribute a small batch of trackable neighbourhood cards and test location-targeted social ads. The illustrative monthly budget is $600: $300 ads, $150 print and $150 photography/content.

Illustrative sales assumptions: The test assumes 100 tracked first purchases and a $6 average ticket, giving $600 of first-purchase revenue and a $6 acquisition cost. If each purchase contributes $3 after variable costs, those first purchases contribute only $300 before the $600 campaign cost. Repeat visits must justify continuation; the plan does not treat revenue as profit.

Measurement and response: The owner reviews redemption, first purchases, repeat visits and queue time weekly. If acquisition costs remain above the contribution expected from repeat purchases, pause the weakest channel and test the offer before increasing spend.

Why it works: The customer, channel costs, sales assumptions and review decision are connected. The example exposes the cash needed to acquire customers rather than assuming that more promotion automatically creates profitable growth. Compare the broader coffee shop sample business plan.

Cleaning company — Clearstep

Customer and offer: Small offices within a practical travel area, with an office manager responsible for recurring cleaning. The offer is a site visit followed by a task-specific proposal.

Channels, budget and owner: The founder will contact suitable offices directly, build referral relationships with property managers and test search ads. The $1,000 monthly cash budget allocates $500 to ads, $300 to outreach tools and $200 to collateral. Founder sales time is recorded separately at an illustrative $600 allocated cost.

Illustrative sales assumptions: Assume 20 qualified enquiries, eight site visits and two new recurring accounts: an enquiry-to-account conversion of 10%. The fully costed CAC is ($1,000 + $600) ÷ 2 = $800 per new account. Two accounts at $600 monthly each add $1,200 of recurring monthly revenue, with collection dates and cancellation assumptions shown separately.

Measurement and response: The founder records enquiries, proposals, wins, sales hours and retention. With an illustrative $240 monthly contribution per account, simple acquisition payback is $800 ÷ $240, about 3.3 months before other overhead and timing effects. Check staffing and travel capacity before accepting more sites.

Why it works: The customer, channel costs, sales assumptions and review decision are connected. The example exposes the cash needed to acquire customers rather than assuming that more promotion automatically creates profitable growth. Compare the broader cleaning company sample business plan.

Ecommerce startup — Loop Shelf

Customer and offer: Households searching for repairable desk accessories, starting with a narrow product range and delivery area. The offer pairs replacement parts with clear fitting instructions.

Channels, budget and owner: The founder will test search ads for specific product needs, publish repair demonstrations and build opt-in email follow-up. A $1,200 monthly budget splits into $700 ads, $300 content and $200 email and store tools.

Illustrative sales assumptions: Assume 1,500 tracked site visits, a 2% purchase conversion and 30 first orders at a $60 average order value: $1,800 revenue before returns. If all 30 are new customers, the simplified cash CAC is $1,200 ÷ 30 = $40. At $24 contribution per order after product, payment and fulfilment costs, first-order contribution totals $720, below acquisition spend.

Measurement and response: The founder reviews contribution after returns, new-customer CAC and repeat orders by cohort. The test stops or changes if the offer cannot recover acquisition costs within the available cash budget. Traffic cannot be scaled beyond inventory and fulfilment capacity.

Why it works: The customer, channel costs, sales assumptions and review decision are connected. The example exposes the cash needed to acquire customers rather than assuming that more promotion automatically creates profitable growth. Compare the broader ecommerce sample business plan.

Build your own marketing plan

For each customer segment, record the offer, channel, monthly budget, responsible person, expected enquiries, conversion rate, average purchase and repeat-purchase assumption. Separate one-time setup costs from recurring costs. State how each assumption will be validated and when the owner will review it.

Carry the same volumes into revenue, acquisition costs into the expense budget and payment timing into cash flow. Include the staff time needed to fulfil sales and service existing customers. Read the CAC guide and contribution margin guide before deciding whether a campaign pays for itself.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not list every platform without a customer reason, count enquiries as signed orders or use revenue as the measure of campaign profit. Keep repeat purchases separate from new customers. Model slower conversion and delayed collections so a missed sales target does not hide a cash shortfall.

Frequently asked questions

What should a business-plan marketing section include?

Include the target customer, offer, positioning, channels, budget, owner, sales assumptions and review measures. Connect those choices to the financial forecast.

Are the example conversion rates recommended targets?

No. All figures are illustrative assumptions. Validate your own rates through research and tests, and include a downside case.

How do I choose a marketing budget?

Estimate the cost of reaching and converting a defined customer group, then check contribution, retention, capacity and available cash. Review actual results before expanding.
