How to Write a CSBFP Business Plan, Step by Step

A practical guide to explaining your business, funding needs and repayment case.

The Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP) helps small businesses access financing through financial institutions by sharing lending risk. The lender provides the money and decides whether to lend.

Your business plan is the main narrative document connecting the proposal, funding request and repayment case. It helps the lender interpret your financial package; it does not replace credit checks or supporting records. ISED requires lenders to assess repayment ability, but does not prescribe one universal business-plan template. Start with the lender's instructions, then use these steps to build a consistent application.

Before you write: check eligibility and proposed uses

Start with two questions: can the business apply, and can the proposed costs be financed? ISED's borrower guidance includes startups and existing small businesses operating in Canada with gross annual revenues of $10 million or less. Farming businesses are excluded. Eligibility to apply does not determine the lending decision.

For term loans, the published categories are commercial real property purchases or improvements; new or used equipment purchases or improvements; new or existing leasehold improvements; intangible assets; working capital costs; and the registration fee. Lines of credit cover working capital costs and the registration fee. Category limits and other conditions apply. Ask the lender to classify each cost before treating it as eligible.

Collect the lender's application checklist, supplier quotations, ownership records and available financial statements before drafting. Keep project costs separate from the amount requested under CSBFP. Some costs may need owner funds or another source. For broader context, our guide to business loans in Canada explains how to organise a borrowing request.

The plan, section by section

The following structure is practical preparation guidance, rather than an official CSBFP document specification. Each section should answer a question the lender needs to resolve.

Step 1: Write the executive summary last

Summarise what the business sells, who buys it, who runs it and what the financing will accomplish. State the requested facility, project cost, proposed owner contribution and repayment source. Use the same amounts as the application and financial schedules.

The lender needs a clear introduction to the transaction. Explain the current stage: a new opening, an acquisition or expansion of an operating business. Identify major dependencies, such as securing premises or completing renovations. Write this section after the detailed work so it summarises evidence already in the plan.

Step 2: Explain the business and ownership

Describe the legal business, ownership interests, location, products and revenue model. Identify who will borrow and who will manage daily operations. Include relevant management experience and explain how gaps in skills will be covered.

The lender is assessing the people and organisation responsible for delivering the forecast. Connect experience to specific responsibilities: purchasing, sales, staffing and financial control. For an acquisition, distinguish the operating business from the assets being bought. Identify other connected businesses so the lender can examine any relevant relationships.

Step 3: Build a market case with traceable evidence

Define the customers within your actual service area and explain why they would buy at your proposed price. Describe competitors, substitutes and your route to customers. Use dated research, observed local conditions and your own trading records where available.

The lender needs to understand the basis for projected sales. Translate demand into practical drivers: enquiries, conversions, repeat purchases, units and average transaction value. Distinguish signed business from expressions of interest. Explain how marketing activity produces sales without assuming that a large national market automatically creates local customers.

Step 4: Show how operations support the forecast

Describe premises, equipment, suppliers, staffing, delivery methods and relevant permits or licences. Explain the sequence from financing to opening or expansion. Identify which costs arise before revenue begins and which commitments depend on other work being completed.

The lender is looking for an operating explanation that fits the numbers. Sales cannot exceed practical capacity without additional resources. Connect opening hours to staffing costs, purchasing to inventory needs and equipment to output. Explain the effect of delays, supplier interruptions or seasonal slowdowns, including how additional cash needs would be covered.

Step 5: Assemble the financial package

Begin with an itemised startup or expansion budget. Use actual quotes where available and label estimates. The table below is a blank planning structure, with no assumed costs or eligibility decisions.

Next, prepare sources and uses of funds. Uses show the complete project budget; sources show proposed borrowing, documented owner funds and other financing. The totals must balance. Identify pending financing separately from confirmed funds, and avoid counting the same owner money twice.

Then build cash flow projections showing when cash enters and leaves the business. Include customer payment delays, inventory purchases, payroll, rent, taxes, owner pay and debt payments. Separate loan proceeds from sales receipts. Add projected income statements and balance sheets in the format and forecast period requested by the lender.

Reconcile opening cash, capital purchases, borrowing and closing balances across the schedules. Attach an assumptions page explaining prices, volumes, margins, payment terms and seasonality. Where proposed loan terms remain uncertain, label the modelling assumptions and update them when the lender provides terms.

The numbers lenders scrutinise

Cash flow coverage: explain whether operating cash can meet expenses and debt payments when due. Profit alone does not show cash available for repayment. Ask how the lender defines repayment coverage and which obligations it includes; do not apply an assumed universal ratio.

Owner contribution: state how much you propose to invest, where it comes from and when it will be available. Identify borrowed contributions and their repayment obligations. Evidence should support the amount shown in sources and uses. Discuss the required contribution with your lender rather than assuming one percentage applies to every request.

Realistic assumptions: show the evidence behind the base forecast and test a plausible downside. What happens if sales build more slowly, a major expense rises or opening is delayed? Explain the resulting cash gap and practical responses. A sensitivity case should change the relevant operating costs and timing as well as revenue.

Common mistakes to catch before submitting

Incomplete personal statements: where the lender requests personal net-worth or financial statements, complete all requested asset, liability and obligation fields. Reconcile supporting balances and explain omissions. These documents support the lender's review; this is not a claim that every CSBFP application has an identical personal-document requirement.

Projections without assumptions: a spreadsheet containing sales growth but no explanation of customer numbers, pricing or capacity is difficult to assess. Make the calculation chain visible and identify the source of each material estimate.

Ineligible costs inside the request: ISED identifies share purchases and assets acquired by a holding company as examples that cannot be financed. Its exclusions are not exhaustive. Keep every project cost visible, but separate costs the lender confirms as eligible from those requiring another funding source.

Conflicting versions: different borrowing amounts in the summary, budget and cash forecast obscure the request. Update the whole package when a quotation or financing assumption changes. Keep dated copies of the evidence behind the final submission.

Checklist recap: assemble the document package

Confirm the final list with your lender. Use this preparation checklist and mark items as applicable, requested or outstanding:

  • Business plan: executive summary, business and ownership, market evidence, operations and repayment explanation.
  • Itemised startup or expansion costs, supplier quotes, equipment specifications and renovation estimates.
  • Balanced sources and uses, evidence of owner funds and details of other proposed financing.
  • Cash flow projections, projected income statements and balance sheets, assumptions and downside analysis.
  • Historical financial statements, interim results, business bank statements and tax records, where available and requested.
  • Requested personal financial or net-worth statements, supporting records and details of personal obligations.
  • Registration or incorporation records, ownership information and relevant management résumés.
  • Applicable lease or purchase agreements, permits, licences and material customer or supplier contracts.
  • Existing debt details and any security or guarantee information requested by the lender.
  • Completed lender application, consistent figures throughout and a list of unresolved items.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a CSBFP business plan be?

Use the length needed to explain the business and support the financing request. Ask the lender about its preferred format. Keep the main narrative readable and place detailed quotes, research and schedules in appendices. The official sources cited here do not set a universal page count.

Can I write it myself?

You can prepare your own draft and financial package. Follow the lender's instructions, substantiate the assumptions and make sure you can explain every figure. Professional help can organise the work, but the owner still needs to understand the business case.

What can the loan be used for?

The official term-loan categories cover commercial real property, equipment, leasehold improvements, intangible assets, working capital and the registration fee, as described above. Lines of credit cover working capital and the registration fee. Ask the lender to confirm each item's eligibility and applicable limits.

How much can I borrow?

ISED publishes a combined maximum of $1.15 million: up to $1 million in term loans and $150,000 in lines of credit. Within term loans, the non-real-property ceiling is $500,000, including at most $150,000 for intangible assets and working capital. These are program ceilings; the lender determines the amount it will lend and checks applicable limits.
