What Lenders Look For in a Business Plan

Connect your funding request, evidence and repayment story using our Benchmarks report's practitioner checklist.

  1. Home
  2. Learning Centre
  3. What lenders look for

A business plan is easier to assess when the financing request, operating story and financial model agree. A polished introduction can invite a closer look, but the supporting evidence must explain how the business will use the money and carry the repayments. Unexplained assumptions leave questions that the founder still needs to answer.

Our Canadian Business Plan Benchmarks 2026 report provides a useful starting point. It combines published Canadian financing research with practitioner commentary about the evidence lenders examine. It is desk research, not a survey of submitted plans. Its lender checklist supplies the framework below; the practical suggestions show how to apply that framework to your own document.

The report does not measure which writing styles receive closer attention or establish a formula for approval. The useful question is whether a reviewer can follow your reasoning and verify the evidence behind it. Start there before spending more time on presentation.

What lenders check first: cash flow and repayment

In our Canadian Business Plan Benchmarks 2026, the practitioner checklist states: Debt-service coverage — can the cash flow carry the payments? This is the first ratio computed.

That is the report's practitioner framing, rather than a measured reading order across lenders. For your plan, the implication is practical: make repayment capacity easy to find. Explain where operating cash comes from, when customers pay, when suppliers and staff must be paid, and how proposed borrowing affects the cash position.

A profit forecast alone leaves timing questions unanswered. Sales can appear in the income statement before the customer settles the invoice. Stock may need to be purchased before it can be sold. Show those movements in the cash-flow forecast and connect them to the repayment assumptions.

Explain what happens if trading is weaker than expected. Identify expenses you could defer, decisions you would reconsider and any additional funding the business might need. Avoid presenting an unsupported rescue contribution as if it were already available. Ask the lender to confirm the repayment terms and coverage expectations relevant to your application.

Use of funds: explain the purpose behind the request

In our Canadian Business Plan Benchmarks 2026, the practitioner checklist states: The use of funds — every dollar traced to a purpose. Vague "working capital" lines get questioned.

Give the requested borrowing a clear job. Describe the equipment, inventory, premises work or operating needs it supports. Separate the project costs from the funding sources, including your own contribution and any financing already arranged. The sources should reconcile with the uses and with the opening position in the forecast.

Working capital needs an explanation just as much as an equipment purchase does. Describe the gap between paying for operations and collecting customer receipts. Use the cash forecast to show why that gap arises and what the requested facility would cover.

Attach current supporting quotes where available and identify estimates that still need confirmation. Explain how the purchases support your operating capacity. An equipment request should connect to the production or service assumptions in the plan, rather than appearing as an isolated shopping list.

Collateral and guarantees: make the support clear

In our Canadian Business Plan Benchmarks 2026, the practitioner checklist states: Collateral and guarantees — what's securing the loan if cash flow stumbles.

Describe the assets involved in the proposed financing and disclose existing borrowing or security commitments that affect the discussion. Keep ownership, outstanding obligations and supporting documents consistent. If an asset valuation is uncertain, label it clearly instead of treating a rough estimate as an agreed lending value.

Security does not replace the explanation of how the business expects to repay. It is a separate part of the financing discussion. Ask the lender what information it needs about collateral and potential guarantees, and obtain advice on commitments you do not understand before agreeing to them.

Your plan should distinguish what you are proposing from what a lender has confirmed. That distinction helps prevent the financial model from relying on financing arrangements that remain under discussion.

Assumption quality: connect market evidence to the forecast

In our Canadian Business Plan Benchmarks 2026, the practitioner checklist states: Assumption quality — documented, sourced, conservative. One invented number poisons the whole projection.

Document how you reached the sales, pricing, margin and cost assumptions. Useful evidence can include supplier quotations, customer enquiries, contracts, observed competitor pricing and your own trading records. Label the source and explain its relevance. A general industry trend does not establish that your business can attract the customers assumed in its forecast.

Make the market section do financial work. Define the customer you intend to serve, explain the problem you solve and describe how customers will find and choose you. Then connect that argument to the sales forecast through operating assumptions such as capacity, customer acquisition and repeat purchases.

Separate confirmed demand from expressions of interest and from your own estimates. Consider whether staffing, premises, equipment and marketing activity can support the proposed sales. Where evidence is limited, acknowledge the uncertainty and show how you would test the assumption. A transparent estimate gives a reviewer something useful to challenge.

Founder credibility: show who will deliver

In our Canadian Business Plan Benchmarks 2026, the practitioner checklist states: Founder credibility — industry experience and personal investment in the deal.

The management section should explain how the business will operate under the proposed team. Connect relevant experience to the work ahead: serving customers, managing suppliers, controlling costs, supervising staff and keeping financial records. Specific responsibilities help the reader assess whether essential work has an owner.

Be open about missing skills. Explain whether you will hire, train, contract support or use an advisor, and reflect those decisions in the budget. A plan that acknowledges a capability gap and explains the response is more useful than an unqualified claim that the team can handle everything.

Describe your proposed investment consistently across the narrative and financial schedules. Distinguish funds already committed from contributions that depend on another event. Personal commitment supports the story, but it should not be presented as a substitute for viable operating assumptions.

The gaps the Benchmarks flags, with a practical fix

The report flags weaknesses through practitioner commentary; it does not rank the most common deficiencies in a sample of plans. These are the gaps its checklist asks founders to address:

  • Unclear repayment capacity. Fix: connect operating cash flow to the proposed payments and explain a weaker trading scenario.
  • Vague use of funds. Fix: replace broad funding labels with explained costs that reconcile to the financing request.
  • Unclear security arrangements. Fix: describe proposed collateral and existing obligations, then confirm requirements with the lender.
  • Unsupported assumptions. Fix: record the source, reasoning and uncertainty behind material forecast inputs.
  • Unexplained founder capability or investment. Fix: connect experience, responsibilities and committed contributions to the operating plan.

Review these together. A staffing decision changes costs; a purchase changes capacity; delayed collections change cash availability. Updating the narrative without updating the model leaves the reviewer with conflicting versions of the business.

A yes/no self-check before you send the plan

Answer each question against the actual document and supporting schedules. Treat a “no” as a revision task, then check that the correction flows through the rest of the plan.

  • Can a reader locate the borrowing request and its purpose easily?
  • Do the funding sources reconcile with the project costs?
  • Does the cash forecast include the proposed loan payments?
  • Have you explained how slower trading would affect cash?
  • Can you trace material forecast assumptions to evidence or clearly labelled estimates?
  • Does customer and competitor research support the sales argument?
  • Can the planned staffing and equipment support the forecast activity?
  • Have you disclosed existing borrowing and described proposed security?
  • Are management responsibilities, skill gaps and founder contributions clear?

Frequently asked questions

What do lenders look at first?

The Benchmarks report's practitioner checklist puts debt-service coverage first: whether cash flow can carry the payments. Make the request, use of funds and repayment case easy to locate. The report does not establish a universal review sequence, so confirm the intended lender's document requirements.

How long should the plan be?

The Benchmarks report does not publish a plan-length benchmark. Aim for a clear main document with supporting evidence organised in appendices. Include enough detail to explain the business, funding request and assumptions, and follow any format or length guidance supplied by your lender.

Do lenders read the whole plan?

The report does not measure how much of a plan lenders read. Make it usable both for an initial review and for detailed questions: clear headings, consistent figures and labelled supporting documents help readers find the evidence they need. Avoid assuming that an appendix will explain an unclear main argument.

What is the most common weakness?

The report does not rank weaknesses by frequency. Its practitioner checklist explicitly warns about vague working-capital descriptions and invented assumptions. Check both, alongside repayment capacity, collateral and founder credibility, without treating any as a statistically established leading problem.

Prepare a plan you can explain

A useful lender-facing plan lets you explain how the business works and defend the assumptions behind the request. It cannot promise approval.
