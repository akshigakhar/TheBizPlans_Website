CIM Preparation: Confidential Information Memorandum

Present your business clearly for a controlled sale process.

A confidential information memorandum (CIM) is the confidential marketing document a seller’s advisor prepares so qualified buyers can evaluate a business for sale. It explains the business, its earnings and the proposed transaction while supporting a controlled sale process. An anonymous teaser usually comes first; the detailed CIM is shared selectively after appropriate confidentiality arrangements, helping avoid premature exposure of the company’s identity and market position.

What a CIM includes — full structure

A confidential information memorandum template can suggest headings, but the substance must come from verified business records. Use this outline to understand what a complete CIM contains. It describes the document’s structure without using real company information or fictional financial figures.

Executive summary and teaser relationship

The executive summary introduces the opportunity, business model, principal strengths, ownership objective and key financial themes. It should help a buyer decide which questions deserve attention next. It belongs inside the confidential document and can identify the business once disclosure is authorized. The earlier teaser gives an anonymous overview with enough context to assess initial fit. Good summaries agree with the detailed sections and avoid unsupported claims about uniqueness, returns or buyer demand.

Company overview and history

Describe the legal and operating structure, ownership, locations and development of the business. Explain important changes such as new facilities, product launches or acquisitions where relevant to present operations. Distinguish the entities or assets being discussed from those outside the proposed sale. A useful history connects milestones to current capabilities and financial trends, rather than listing dates without context. The buyer should understand what the company actually owns, controls and relies upon.

Products and services

Explain what customers purchase, how pricing works and which revenue streams are recurring, contracted or project-based. Describe delivery obligations, product differentiation and any material intellectual property, licences or supplier dependencies. Separate established offerings from concepts still under development. Good presentation connects the revenue mix to the financial schedules and explains whether margins vary between offerings. Readers should be able to see how the business earns money without needing to interpret internal product names.

Market and industry position

Define the customers, geography and industry segments the company serves. Explain competitive alternatives, barriers to entry and relevant market trends with dated, attributable sources. Distinguish market evidence from management’s interpretation. A strong section acknowledges competitive pressure and regulatory exposure alongside the company’s position. It avoids treating a broad industry growth forecast as proof that the business will achieve the same growth. Market claims should support the operating story rather than replace company evidence.

Operations

Describe facilities, equipment, technology, suppliers, capacity and the workflow from order to payment. Explain leased versus owned assets and any material renewal, maintenance or capital-spending needs. Show where operations depend on a particular location, system or supplier. Good disclosure helps a buyer understand what must continue after a transfer and what may require consent. The operating description should match staffing, margins and capital expenditure assumptions used elsewhere in the CIM.

Management and staff structure

Show the organization, responsibilities, management depth and the owner’s day-to-day involvement. Explain which functions depend on the seller and what transition support is proposed, without assuming that employees have agreed to remain. Distinguish current responsibilities from intended post-sale arrangements. A good section makes key-person dependence visible and identifies questions for later diligence. Personal employee information should be limited to what is appropriate for the disclosure stage; discuss naming and access with the seller’s advisor.

Customer concentration and relationships

Explain customer segments, repeat purchasing, sales channels and the extent to which revenue depends on major accounts. Concentration should be measured consistently over clearly identified periods; revenue concentration and profitability concentration may tell different stories. Describe contract duration, renewal patterns and transfer restrictions where relevant. Good presentation discloses dependence honestly while explaining relationship history. Early versions can use anonymized customer references, with identifying details released separately when authorized and necessary for buyer diligence.

Financial performance and adjusted or normalised earnings

Present historical income statements, balance-sheet information and cash-flow context with the periods, currency and accounting basis clearly identified. Explain changes in revenue, margins, working capital and capital expenditure. Reconcile the narrative with the underlying statements and distinguish historical results from projections. Our EBITDA guide explains one commonly discussed earnings measure, including why it differs from cash flow.

Sellers normalise earnings to help buyers assess the costs of operating the business under continuing ownership. Owner compensation, personal expenses recorded through the company or genuinely non-recurring costs can distort that view. An owner add-back is a proposed adjustment, not automatically an accepted increase in earnings. Replacement management costs and expenses a buyer would still incur must also be considered.

A good adjustment schedule starts with reported earnings, lists each proposed adjustment, explains the reason and identifies supporting records. It distinguishes documented adjustments from items still requiring confirmation. The CPA works through the presentation with the seller and relevant accountant; buyers and their advisors assess it independently. A CIM is not an audit or a substitute for financial due diligence.

Growth opportunities

Describe credible opportunities such as additional capacity, new channels or improved utilization, and explain the resources each would require. Separate initiatives already underway from options a future owner might choose. Link projections to stated assumptions, investment needs and constraints; identify where execution depends on people, approvals or spending. Good presentation lets a buyer evaluate the logic without presenting potential synergies as certain results. Our financial modelling services address a separate model when the scope requires one.

Transaction rationale and process

Explain why the owner is considering a sale, what ownership interest or assets are under discussion and which matters remain open. Include the advisor’s contact route, information-request procedure and next steps agreed for the process. Distinguish seller preferences from negotiated terms. A useful section explains how questions and additional disclosure will be managed without implying that the CIM itself is an offer, purchase agreement or guarantee. Legal and tax terms belong with the appropriate transaction specialists.

When you need a CIM in mergers and acquisitions

A CIM is useful when selling a business through a broker or advisor who needs a consistent information package for qualified buyers. It can also support approaches to strategic buyers who need to understand the company’s activities, relationships and operating fit. Giving authorized readers the same core information reduces conflicting explanations across a sale process.

Lenders or investors reviewing a proposed acquisition may also use the CIM to understand the target business. Their financing assessment usually requires additional information about the buyer, proposed capital structure and repayment assumptions. A seller’s CIM does not replace the buyer’s financing case or independent diligence.

For a very small, straightforward sale, an anonymous teaser followed by a simple financial package may be enough. Discuss the business’s complexity, confidentiality needs and intended readers before commissioning a full document. If the immediate need is to organize records and understand earnings before going to market, explore our fractional CFO services for owners preparing a business for sale.

CIM vs. business plan vs. teaser

These documents answer different questions. A CIM explains an existing business offered for sale, a business plan develops the operating and funding case, and a teaser introduces an opportunity before detailed disclosure. A transaction can require more than one.

How CIM preparation works

The engagement follows the site’s discovery, information gathering, financial analysis, drafting and review approach. We confirm the intended reader and deliverables before work begins, then connect the narrative and financial schedules around the actual business.

  1. Discovery and document gathering. Clarify the sale objective, advisor involvement and available financial and operating records. Identify evidence gaps and agree information-sharing arrangements.
  2. Financial normalisation work with the CPA. Review historical performance, discuss proposed owner add-backs and reconcile the earnings presentation with source records. Coordinate unresolved accounting questions with the responsible accountant.
  3. Drafting. Develop the company narrative, market context, operating explanation and transaction sections alongside the financial schedules. Keep assumptions visible and flag claims that need confirmation.
  4. Seller review. Check business facts, disclosure boundaries and consistency between narrative and numbers. Incorporate corrections and resolve material questions with the seller and relevant advisors.
  5. Buyer-ready document. Complete the agreed document for authorized distribution through the seller’s process. The seller and advisor retain responsibility for recipient selection and subsequent disclosure.

Frequently asked questions

What is a confidential information memorandum?

A confidential information memorandum, or CIM, is a detailed document used to present a business for sale to qualified buyers. It explains the company, market, operations, financial performance and proposed transaction process. It is generally shared selectively after confidentiality arrangements, following an anonymous teaser where appropriate.

What should a CIM include?

A CIM should include an executive summary, company history, products and services, market position, operations, management, customer concentration, financial performance, explained earnings adjustments, growth opportunities and transaction rationale. The structure section above describes each part. Content should be supported by business records, with historical results clearly separated from projections.

Who prepares a CIM: the owner, broker or advisor?

An owner can assemble information and contribute a draft, while a broker or transaction advisor often leads preparation and distribution. Accountants and financial writers may prepare the financial schedules and narrative. Responsibilities vary by engagement. The owner verifies business facts; the advisor coordinates the sale process. The Biz Plans prepares the document within the agreed scope.

How is a CIM different from a business plan?

A CIM presents an existing business for a potential sale, emphasizing historical performance, operating dependencies and the transaction context. A business plan explains strategy, execution, financial projections and funding requirements. Some material overlaps, but the audience and decision differ. An acquisition buyer may need a separate business plan for financing.
